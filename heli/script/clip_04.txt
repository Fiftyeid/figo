To see why that matters, picture a helicopter rotor from above. In a hover, every blade moves through the air at the same speed. On a helicopter like the Lynx, the blade tips are already doing almost 800 kilometers per hour, just spinning in place. Now fly forward. On one side, the advancing blade swings into the wind, so the helicopter's speed is added to it. On the other side, the retreating blade swings away from the wind, so the helicopter's speed is taken away. At 400 kilometers per hour, the advancing tip is doing nearly 1,200, right at the edge of the sound barrier. The retreating tip is crawling along at under 400. And close to the hub, something even stranger happens. The air actually flows over the retreating blade backwards. On G-Lynx, that reverse flow covered roughly half of the blade. That creates two problems at once. On the advancing side, shock waves start to form on the blade tips, and drag, vibration and noise shoot up. On the retreating side, the slow blade has to tilt to a steeper angle to make the same lift as the fast one, or the helicopter would simply roll over. Tilt it too far, and the airflow tears away from the blade. That's called retreating blade stall. The nose pitches up, the helicopter rolls, and the pilot can lose control.
