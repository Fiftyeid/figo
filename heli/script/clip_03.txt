The engineers fitted uprated Rolls-Royce Gem 60 engines, cleared to run at their emergency rating for about forty-five percent more power. They added water-methanol injection for an extra boost, reshaped the exhausts to push out some bonus thrust, and borrowed tail surfaces from another Westland helicopter to keep it stable. But the real secret was on top. G-Lynx carried experimental rotor blades called BERP blades, with strange paddle-shaped tips, swept back like the wing of a jet. Westland had developed them together with the UK Ministry of Defence, to make rotors faster and more efficient. On the evening of August 11th, test pilot Trevor Egginton and flight test engineer Derek Clews lined up on a fifteen-kilometer course over the Somerset Levels, near Glastonbury, and flew it twice, in opposite directions, so the wind couldn't help them. Their average was 400.87 kilometers per hour. That's 249 miles per hour. On one of the runs, they touched almost 413. It smashed the Hind's record, and it still stands today. The FAI, the body that certifies aviation records, still lists it as the absolute speed record for helicopters. So officially, the fastest helicopter flight ever recorded was made by a military Lynx. And there's one detail that explains why nobody has beaten it. At 400 kilometers per hour, the tips of its rotor blades were moving at Mach 0.97. That's ninety-seven percent of the speed of sound.
