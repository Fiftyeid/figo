So why not spin the rotor faster? Then the advancing tip hits the sound barrier even sooner. Slow it down? Then the retreating blade stalls sooner. Engineers are trapped between the two. That's why almost every helicopter in service tops out around 300 kilometers per hour, and why G-Lynx needed exotic blades just to touch 400. It's not an engine problem. It's a wall. So engineers asked a simple question. What if the rotor didn't have to do everything? A normal helicopter's rotor has three jobs at once: it lifts the aircraft, pulls it forward, and steers it. A compound helicopter hands some of that work to other parts. Wings take over some of the lift at high speed, and a propeller does the pushing, so the rotor can relax. The US Army tried this back in 1967, with the Lockheed AH-56 Cheyenne, a futuristic gunship with stubby wings and a pusher propeller in its tail. It reached about 215 knots in level flight, close to 400 kilometers per hour, in the late 1960s. But it was expensive, complicated and late, and in August 1972 the Army canceled it. Remember that name. It's coming back.
