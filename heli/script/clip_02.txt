In the 1970s, the Soviet Union built a helicopter unlike anything in the West. The Mil Mi-24, known to NATO as the Hind, was a heavily armored gunship that could also carry eight soldiers in the back. Its crews nicknamed it the crocodile. Others called it a flying tank. Tanks are not supposed to be fast. But the Hind had a sleek body, retractable landing gear, and two powerful turbine engines. And on September 21st, 1978, the Soviets set out to prove a point. They took a modified Hind, removed its stub wings to cut drag, gave it the cover name A-10, and flew it over a measured course near Moscow at 368.4 kilometers per hour. That's 229 miles per hour, and it was the new absolute speed record for helicopters. Its modern descendant, the Mi-35M, is still listed among the fastest combat helicopters flying today. And for eight years, the Soviets owned the title. Then the British decided to take it back. The Westland Lynx was a small, nimble helicopter flown by the British Army and the Royal Navy. In 1986, Westland took its own company demonstrator, registration G-Lynx, and turned it into a rotor-powered dragster.
