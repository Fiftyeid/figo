# Voice-Over Script — World’s Fastest Military Helicopter Explained

Final runtime: 13:07 — 13 sections — Voice: English documentary narrator

## Intro  `(00:01 -> 01:10)`

On the evening of August 11th, 1986, a British military helicopter tore across the flat fields of Somerset, in the south-west of England, at just over 400 kilometers per hour. About 250 miles per hour. That's not the strange part. The strange part is what happened next. Nothing. Forty years later, no conventional helicopter has officially flown faster.

So what is the fastest military helicopter in the world? Ask the internet, and you'll get four different answers. A twin-rotor heavy lifter that first flew in 1961. A Soviet gunship nicknamed the flying tank. That British record-breaker. And a strange new machine the US Army just picked to replace the Black Hawk, which might not even count as a helicopter.

They can't all be right. Or can they? To find out, we need to understand the invisible wall that every helicopter slams into, and the sixty-year race to break through it.

Let's start with the answer you'll find first.

## Answer 1: CH-47F Chinook  `(01:11 -> 02:36)`

Answer number one comes from Guinness World Records. According to Guinness, the fastest military helicopter on the planet is the Boeing CH-47F Chinook, with a top speed of 315 kilometers per hour, or 196 miles per hour.

At first, that sounds like a joke. The Chinook is basically a flying school bus. It can carry around fifty-five troops, or sling an artillery piece underneath, and its basic design first flew back in 1961. More than twelve hundred have been built, and over twenty countries still fly them.

So how does a flying bus keep up with attack helicopters? Look at the rotors. Most helicopters spend a chunk of their engine power on a small tail rotor, whose only job is to stop the body from spinning the opposite way. The Chinook doesn't need one. Its two big rotors turn in opposite directions and cancel each other out, so all of that power goes into lift and speed.

But there's a catch. Not everyone measures top speed the same way. Some manufacturers quote true airspeed, some quote indicated airspeed, and some quote the never-exceed speed, a limit the pilot should never cross. On paper, a few helicopters beat the Chinook, depending on whose spreadsheet you read. Which brings us to answer number two.

## Answer 2: Mi-24 Hind  `(02:36 -> 03:44)`

In the 1970s, the Soviet Union built a helicopter unlike anything in the West. The Mil Mi-24, known to NATO as the Hind, was a heavily armored gunship that could also carry eight soldiers in the back. Its crews nicknamed it the crocodile. Others called it a flying tank.

Tanks are not supposed to be fast. But the Hind had a sleek body, retractable landing gear, and two powerful turbine engines. And on September 21st, 1978, the Soviets set out to prove a point. They took a modified Hind, removed its stub wings to cut drag, gave it the cover name A-10, and flew it over a measured course near Moscow at 368.4 kilometers per hour. That's 229 miles per hour, and it was the new absolute speed record for helicopters.

Its modern descendant, the Mi-35M, is still listed among the fastest combat helicopters flying today. And for eight years, the Soviets owned the title. Then the British decided to take it back.

## Answer 3: G-Lynx  `(03:44 -> 04:00)`

The Westland Lynx was a small, nimble helicopter flown by the British Army and the Royal Navy. In 1986, Westland took its own company demonstrator, registration G-Lynx, and turned it into a rotor-powered dragster.

## Building G-Lynx  `(04:00 -> 04:42)`

The engineers fitted uprated Rolls-Royce Gem 60 engines, cleared to run at their emergency rating for about forty-five percent more power. They added water-methanol injection for an extra boost, reshaped the exhausts to push out some bonus thrust, and borrowed tail surfaces from another Westland helicopter to keep it stable. But the real secret was on top. G-Lynx carried experimental rotor blades called BERP blades, with strange paddle-shaped tips, swept back like the wing of a jet. Westland had developed them together with the UK Ministry of Defence, to make rotors faster and more efficient.

## The record run  `(04:42 -> 05:40)`

On the evening of August 11th, test pilot Trevor Egginton and flight test engineer Derek Clews lined up on a fifteen-kilometer course over the Somerset Levels, near Glastonbury, and flew it twice, in opposite directions, so the wind couldn't help them. Their average was 400.87 kilometers per hour. That's 249 miles per hour. On one of the runs, they touched almost 413.

It smashed the Hind's record, and it still stands today. The FAI, the body that certifies aviation records, still lists it as the absolute speed record for helicopters. So officially, the fastest helicopter flight ever recorded was made by a military Lynx.

And there's one detail that explains why nobody has beaten it. At 400 kilometers per hour, the tips of its rotor blades were moving at Mach 0.97. That's ninety-seven percent of the speed of sound.

## The wall  `(05:41 -> 06:35)`

To see why that matters, picture a helicopter rotor from above. In a hover, every blade moves through the air at the same speed. On a helicopter like the Lynx, the blade tips are already doing almost 800 kilometers per hour, just spinning in place.

Now fly forward. On one side, the advancing blade swings into the wind, so the helicopter's speed is added to it. On the other side, the retreating blade swings away from the wind, so the helicopter's speed is taken away. At 400 kilometers per hour, the advancing tip is doing nearly 1,200, right at the edge of the sound barrier. The retreating tip is crawling along at under 400. And close to the hub, something even stranger happens. The air actually flows over the retreating blade backwards. On G-Lynx, that reverse flow covered roughly half of the blade.

## Retreating blade stall  `(06:35 -> 07:06)`

That creates two problems at once. On the advancing side, shock waves start to form on the blade tips, and drag, vibration and noise shoot up. On the retreating side, the slow blade has to tilt to a steeper angle to make the same lift as the fast one, or the helicopter would simply roll over. Tilt it too far, and the airflow tears away from the blade. That's called retreating blade stall. The nose pitches up, the helicopter rolls, and the pilot can lose control.

## The trap  `(07:06 -> 07:32)`

So why not spin the rotor faster? Then the advancing tip hits the sound barrier even sooner. Slow it down? Then the retreating blade stalls sooner. Engineers are trapped between the two. That's why almost every helicopter in service tops out around 300 kilometers per hour, and why G-Lynx needed exotic blades just to touch 400. It's not an engine problem. It's a wall.

## Compound helicopters  `(07:33 -> 08:23)`

So engineers asked a simple question. What if the rotor didn't have to do everything?

A normal helicopter's rotor has three jobs at once: it lifts the aircraft, pulls it forward, and steers it. A compound helicopter hands some of that work to other parts. Wings take over some of the lift at high speed, and a propeller does the pushing, so the rotor can relax.

The US Army tried this back in 1967, with the Lockheed AH-56 Cheyenne, a futuristic gunship with stubby wings and a pusher propeller in its tail. It reached about 215 knots in level flight, close to 400 kilometers per hour, in the late 1960s. But it was expensive, complicated and late, and in August 1972 the Army canceled it. Remember that name. It's coming back.

## X2, X3, Defiant, Racer  `(08:24 -> 10:05)`

Decades later, Sikorsky came at the problem from a different angle. Its X2 demonstrator had two rigid rotors stacked on top of each other, spinning in opposite directions. That means there's always an advancing blade on both sides of the aircraft, so the retreating blades don't have to carry the load. At high speed, the X2 slowed its rotors to keep the tips below the sound barrier, while a propeller in the tail did the pushing. On September 15th, 2010, it reached 250 knots in level flight. That's about 463 kilometers per hour, much faster than G-Lynx, but the record was never made official. The team still won the Collier Trophy, one of the biggest prizes in American aviation. Three years later, Eurocopter's X3, with short wings and two propellers, pushed that unofficial mark to 255 knots, around 472 kilometers per hour.

Sikorsky turned the X2 idea into military prototypes. First the S-97 Raider, a light scout. Then, together with Boeing, the much bigger SB-1 Defiant, built to replace the Black Hawk. In October 2021, Defiant hit 247 knots, about 457 kilometers per hour, in level flight, and its test pilot said there was still engine power left. In Europe, Airbus's Racer demonstrator has since reached 444 kilometers per hour.

The wall had been broken. So why isn't the Army flying any of these today?

## Answer 4: MV-75 Cheyenne II  `(10:06 -> 11:44)`

Because a completely different idea beat them all. Instead of helping the rotor, why not turn it into a propeller?

That's a tiltrotor. It takes off like a helicopter, then tilts its rotors forward and flies like an airplane, with wings carrying the weight. The US Marines have flown the V-22 Osprey this way for years. And in December 2017, Bell flew a new and much faster tiltrotor for the Army, the V-280 Valor. In testing, it reached 305 knots. That's about 565 kilometers per hour, or 350 miles per hour, faster than any helicopter has ever flown. The production version is designed to carry fourteen soldiers at more than 300 miles per hour.

In December 2022, the Army chose the V-280 over Sikorsky and Boeing's Defiant, in what was described as its biggest helicopter decision in forty years. Then, in February 2024, it canceled its future scout helicopter program too, the one the Raider was built for, saying drones had changed aerial reconnaissance. The fastest compound helicopters ever built were left without a buyer.

And in April 2026, the Army gave its new aircraft an official name: the MV-75 Cheyenne II. It honors the Cheyenne tribes, and it's a nod to that canceled AH-56 Cheyenne, the Army's first big bet on speed. More than half a century later, the name Cheyenne gets a second chance.

## The verdict  `(11:45 -> 13:05)`

So, what is the world's fastest military helicopter? It depends on what you call a helicopter.

If you want the official title for a military helicopter in service, Guinness says it's the CH-47F Chinook, at 315 kilometers per hour. If you want the fastest helicopter flight ever certified, it's G-Lynx, at 400.87, a record that's now forty years old. If you count experimental compound helicopters, the X2 and Defiant went faster still, but never officially. And if you just want the fastest machine the Army will use to carry soldiers into battle, it's the MV-75 Cheyenne II, which isn't really a helicopter at all.

Every one of them is fighting the same enemy: a spinning blade that can't go faster than sound on one side, and can't slow down without stalling on the other.

As for the record holder, G-Lynx is still around. It sits in a helicopter museum in Weston-super-Mare, not far from the course where it made history, still waiting for a conventional helicopter fast enough to take its crown.
