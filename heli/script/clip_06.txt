Decades later, Sikorsky came at the problem from a different angle. Its X2 demonstrator had two rigid rotors stacked on top of each other, spinning in opposite directions. That means there's always an advancing blade on both sides of the aircraft, so the retreating blades don't have to carry the load. At high speed, the X2 slowed its rotors to keep the tips below the sound barrier, while a propeller in the tail did the pushing. On September 15th, 2010, it reached 250 knots in level flight. That's about 463 kilometers per hour, much faster than G-Lynx, but the record was never made official. The team still won the Collier Trophy, one of the biggest prizes in American aviation. Three years later, Eurocopter's X3, with short wings and two propellers, pushed that unofficial mark to 255 knots, around 472 kilometers per hour. Sikorsky turned the X2 idea into military prototypes. First the S-97 Raider, a light scout. Then, together with Boeing, the much bigger SB-1 Defiant, built to replace the Black Hawk. In October 2021, Defiant hit 247 knots, about 457 kilometers per hour, in level flight, and its test pilot said there was still engine power left. In Europe, Airbus's Racer demonstrator has since reached 444 kilometers per hour. The wall had been broken. So why isn't the Army flying any of these today?
