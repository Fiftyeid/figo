## [0:00-0:20] HOOK
ON SCREEN: Title card. Big figure: "4,959 manned aircraft, US Air Force, 2023" [2].
VO: On the figures we can verify, the US Air Force has the most aircraft of the four US military branches: 4,959 manned, in 2023. But ask how many aircraft the US military has, and the answer changes with the rules. This video shows why counting is harder than it looks.

## [0:20-1:35] THE COUNTER RACE
ON SCREEN: Bars grow in turn. Label on the race: "manned aircraft, 2023". Source chip under each bar. Caveat chip: "Air Force and Army: 2023. Navy + Marine Corps: FY2022."
VO: Rules: manned aircraft only, fixed-wing and helicopters. Drones stay out of the bars. Every bar shows its year. Air Force: 4,959, from its 2024 almanac as of September 30, 2023 [1, 2]. Army: about 3,900, from the Congressional Budget Office for 2023 [24]. The bars share a year, but the two sources define the count slightly differently. Read the gap as an approximate measure. The Air Force leads the Army by more than a thousand aircraft on this measure [48]. Every number on screen matches a row in the sources file. The Navy and Marine Corps are reported together as one combined figure, about 4,000 for fiscal 2022 [28]. That card has a different year and a combined scope, so its years and definitions cannot be ranked against these bars. Treat it as a separate question, not as a missing bar.

## [1:35-3:20] AIR FORCE
ON SCREEN (a new visual every 30 to 45 seconds):
- 1:35: Total bar, 5,286. Label: "total inventory including unmanned aircraft and targets, 2023". Split: 4,959 manned | 327 unmanned and targets [1, 2, 16].
- 2:05: Fighters and attack, 2,093 [4]. Stacked: F-16 family 862 [17], F-35A 408 [20], A-10C 261 [46], F-22A 185 [47].
- 2:35: Bombers, 141 [3]. B-52H 76 [40], B-1B 45 [41], B-2A 20 [42]. Transport: C-17A 222 [43], C-130J 151 [44].
- 2:55: Trainers, 1,102 [10]. T-38 family 495 [18], T-6A 442 [19].
- 3:10: Unmanned layer: MQ-9A 244 [13], RQ-4B 9 [14], MQ-1B 1 [15], aerial targets 73 [11]. Total 327 [16].
VO: The Air Force's five core functions are air superiority, global strike, rapid global mobility, intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance, and command and control [39]. Its total inventory is 5,286 aircraft, including unmanned aircraft and targets [1]. That is the second view, and it is not the race figure. The biggest manned group is fighters and attack aircraft, at 2,093 [4], and the F-16 family makes up 862 of them [17]. The F-35A adds 408 [20]. The bomber and transport layers are on screen. Bombers are the long-range strike group. The Congressional Research Service notes that Air Force cargo aircraft carry troops, and that aeromedical transports evacuate injured forces [39]. Tankers are the quiet part of the count. Fighters and bombers need fuel in the air, and the KC-135 family has 376 aircraft [21]. Trainers are a large group, with 1,102 aircraft [10]. Pilot-training aircraft count the same way as combat aircraft in this inventory, because the almanac counts aircraft assigned for training as well as operations. The 4,959 manned figure also includes 25 unpowered gliders, which I count as manned [12]. The unmanned layer, including MQ-9 Reapers, is shown separately, and it is part of the 5,286 total. The race leaves this layer out, so the 4,959 manned figure is not the same as the 5,286 total.

## [3:20-4:40] ARMY
ON SCREEN: "Army manned, about 3,900 (2023, CBO)" [24]. Types, approximate: H-60 about 2,100 [53]; AH-64 about 700 [54]; UH-72 about 500 [55]; H-47 about 400 [56]; manned fixed-wing about 200 [57]. Second-view card: "Army including drones, 2023: about 4,600 (3,900 manned + about 700 large unmanned systems; derived)" [25, 26], beside "Air Force total including unmanned aircraft and targets, 2023: 5,286" [1]. Caveat card: "CBO's Army data exclude smaller tactical drones" [52].
VO: The Army's mission is to find, fix and destroy enemy forces through fire and maneuver, supported by air assault, attack, reconnaissance, special operations and sustainment aircraft [27]. CBO counts about 3,900 manned Army aircraft in 2023, most of them helicopters [24]. CBO's figures are rounded, so each one is spoken with 'about'. The rest are mostly fixed-wing C-12 Huron passenger aircraft, CBO reports [57]. The largest fleet is the H-60 Black Hawk [60]. CBO reports that the manned count has generally declined since 2000, mainly because older rotary-wing types were retired [51]. CBO also counts about 700 large unmanned aerial systems [25], and adding them gives about 4,600 [26]. That is the second view. It sets the Army total against the Air Force total including unmanned aircraft and targets, both labelled 2023. CBO's data also leave out smaller tactical drones, so the two sources are not identical in scope. Drones sit in a second view because a drone and a manned fighter are different kinds of aircraft to count, and a headline should not change because one source counts its drones differently. Read the Army and Air Force totals as two labelled second-view figures, not as a finer ranking.

## [4:40-5:55] NAVY AND MARINE CORPS
ON SCREEN: Combined card: "Navy + Marine Corps (combined, FY2022): about 4,000. The source reports them together. Not split. Not ranked." [28]. Navy role line: strike; maritime patrol; ISR; electronic attack; vertical lift; defense [34]. Marine line: six functions [35].
VO: Naval aviation flies from aircraft carriers. Navy aircraft support carrier air wings with strike, maritime patrol, intelligence surveillance, electronic attack, vertical lift and defense missions [34]. Navy aviation is built around carrier air wings [34]. Marine aviation provides fires, fire support and mobility to Marine forces, through six functions that include air reconnaissance, anti-air warfare and assault support [35]. The two services are reported as one total: about 4,000 aircraft for fiscal year 2022 [28]. No verified source gives a Navy figure or a Marine figure on its own, so I won't estimate either. That is why this is one combined card, not two bars. Its year and scope differ from the other bars, so it cannot be ranked against them. The original Pentagon source is not publicly available, so the figure comes through a Congressional Research Service summary, and the full citation is in the description. The limit is the source's, not this video's: the source reports two services as one number. That is also why the description lists the full source chain.

## [5:55-6:35] CONTEXT (scale check only)
ON SCREEN: "FlightGlobal, 2026 World Air Forces Directory (public summary): just over 52,200 in-service aircraft, 161 nations [36, 37]. Scale check, not a ranking."
VO: For scale only: a public summary of FlightGlobal's 2026 World Air Forces directory puts military aircraft worldwide at just over 52,200, across 161 nations. That figure uses its own in-service definition, and the full directory is paywalled. So it is a scale check, not a ranking. Its value is the scale of the field, not a country-by-country comparison.

## [6:35-8:00] THE ANSWER
ON SCREEN: Final recap frame, four cards:
- "Air Force: 4,959 manned, 2023"
- "Army: about 3,900 manned, 2023"
- "Navy + Marine Corps: about 4,000 combined, FY2022 (not ranked)"
- "Second view: Air Force 5,286 total incl. unmanned aircraft and targets | Army about 4,600 incl. drones, 2023"
End card: "Sources, years and excluded figures are in the description."
VO: So, how many aircraft does the US military have? It depends on the rules. The question has a clear answer under the rules shown, and a different answer under other rules. That is the point of this video. Counting only manned aircraft, the Air Force has 4,959 in 2023, and the Army has about 3,900. Counting unmanned aircraft and targets too, the Air Force total is 5,286, and the Army's is about 4,600. The Navy and Marine Corps are one combined figure for 2022, so they cannot be ranked against these bars. On the figures we can verify, the Air Force comes out first, and it leads the Army on both views. Here is how to read any number in this video: check the year, check whether drones are counted, and check whether the bar is one branch or a combined card. A same-year, same-definition count for the Navy and Marine Corps would change the picture, and I would update the video if one is published. The sources, the years, and the figures left out are in the description. Tell me in the comments which counting rule you would use, and why.
