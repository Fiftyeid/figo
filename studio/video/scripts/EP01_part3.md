# THE LAST NIGHT OF CONSTANTINOPLE — PART III
## Chapters VIII – XIII (verbatim narration)

### VIII · THE EMPEROR'S LAST WALK  [13:00 · ≈1,350 words]

[Footsteps on stone, slow and far apart. Wind in a high place.]

There is a story about Constantine the Eleventh that every schoolbook in Greece still tells, and that most historians think is partly true.

It goes like this: on the night before the city fell, he was offered a ship.

[Pause.]

Let us take that seriously for a moment, because it is the kind of offer that most of us imagine we would accept. There was still a boat. There was still water. The war was lost, and everyone in the room knew it. He was the emperor of nothing, and a ship could take him to Italy, or to the Morea, or to anywhere else in the world where a man with a famous name might be received politely and given a small pension.

He said no.

[Soft pause.]

Why?

The answer the chroniclers give is duty. Or faith. Or honour. Those are good words, and I do not doubt that he felt them. But I want to offer something smaller, because I think the truth is usually smaller.

He had been emperor for four years. In four years he had done nothing but lose things: land, money, allies, time. The city was all he had left to be emperor of.

If he left, he would have stopped being the emperor, and become a man with a title and no place.

And there is a particular kind of tiredness that comes at the end of a long failure, when the only thing you have left is that you did not walk away from it.

[Pause.]

So he stayed. And on the evening of the twenty-eighth of May, he did something that kings do not usually do.

He went for a walk.

[Soft pause.]

Let us follow him.

He is not in armour. He is not on a horse. He is dressed as a man of his rank would dress, but simply, with a cloak against the evening, and he is walking through his own city.

Behind him, a little way back, are a few of his officers. Some of them are carrying lamps.

[Pause.]

The streets are not arranged for him. Nobody has swept them. There is a dog in the road, in the place we left him, and the emperor steps around him.

That is the detail I want you to keep.

[Soft pause.]

He walks down to the great church first, and goes in, and the service is going on, and we have already been inside, so let us wait for him outside, in the square.

When he comes out, his face is wet.

The chroniclers say this. I do not know if it is true. Men write down what they need to write down, and a crying emperor is a useful thing to have in a story.

But let us allow it. It has been a long four years.

[Pause.]

And then he goes to the wall.

This is the part that is hardest to imagine, and the part that matters most.

He walks the length of the land walls — all of them, or nearly all, over five kilometres of stone — and he stops at the places where the guns have been working. He speaks to the men. Not to the officers: to the men. The ones who have been sitting in the dust for weeks, eating their bread on the parapet, mending their boots.

He asks them to hold.

He says it plainly. He does not promise them that help is coming, because there is no help coming, and he is not a man who has ever been good at lying. He tells them that everything that can be done has been done, and that what remains is one more day.

And he asks them to forgive him.

[Pause.]

I want to sit with that sentence for a moment, because it is not a normal thing for an emperor to say.

Forgive me.

Forgive me that the money is gone. Forgive me that the ships were not enough. Forgive me that the walls are old and the guns are new and that we have been doing arithmetic against an army for two months and losing.

Men are not usually asked to forgive their rulers. They are asked to obey them, or to die for them, or to pay for them.

Asking is different. Asking puts you on the same level as the person you are asking.

[Soft pause.]

Now, let us leave the wall, and let us go back up, to the highest part of the city, where the old palace stands.

He does not go to the palace. There is nothing there now but empty rooms and a few servants and a great deal of damage from a fire years ago.

He goes instead to a terrace that overlooks the water, and he stands there for a while.

[Long pause. Wind.]

Let me describe what he would have seen, because it is the same view we took earlier, from the tower with the open shutter, and it is worth seeing again.

Below: the dark roofs, and the lamps, and the streets where a dog is asleep and a cup is tied to a fountain.

Further down: the harbour, the chain, the masts of the enemy ships inside it, the small fleet of the last of the empire.

Beyond: the water, and the shore on the other side, and the hills, and above the hills, a very thin line of light where the moon is either rising or setting, and it is impossible to tell which.

And behind him: the great church, dark now, with the great dome a slightly different shade of dark than the sky.

[Pause.]

He would have been able to name every single thing in that view.

That is what it means to be the emperor of a city for four years. You learn the names. Not the grand ones — the small ones. The name of the harbour. The name of the street that floods in winter. The name of the man who keeps the gate that is not really a gate any more.

He stood there for some time. The chroniclers do not say how long.

[Soft pause.]

And then he went to bed.

[Pause.]

I want to end this chapter with that, because it is the most human thing in the story, and because it is where I would like to leave him.

A man who knows that tomorrow is the end does not stay up all night. He puts his cloak on a hook, and he lies down, and he tries to sleep, and probably fails, and then sleeps after all, because the body asks for it and the body usually wins.

He sleeps for a few hours, in a city that is also sleeping.

And that — that ordinary, unremarkable, unfairly short night of sleep — is the last night of the Roman Empire.

[Long pause. Let it breathe.]

### IX · THE HOURS BEFORE DAWN  [13:00 · ≈1,250 words]

[Almost nothing. A very low room tone. One candle, far off.]

There is a part of the night that nobody writes about.

It is not the evening, when everything is still possible. It is not the morning, when everything is decided. It is the hours in between — three, and four, and five — when the city is asleep, and the guns are quiet, and nothing at all is happening.

Let us spend some time there. It is the quietest place in this whole story, and, in a way, it is where we have been going all along.

[Pause.]

If you have ever been awake at three in the morning, you know what this is like.

The world is not silent. It only sounds silent, because your ears have adjusted. There is a dog, somewhere, turning over. There is a rope against a mast. There is the small, uneven breathing of a city: a door, a cough, a shutter.

And above it, in this city, on this night, there is one other sound.

Somewhere out in the dark, beyond the walls, a man is working on the gun.

[Soft pause.]

We do not know his name. He is one of perhaps a hundred men who have been working on it for days. He is probably young. He has been awake longer than he should be, and he is doing something with his hands that requires all of his attention, in the light of one lamp, in the cold, in the dark.

He is not thinking about history. He is thinking about a ring of iron, and whether it will sit flush, and whether the crack will hold.

[Pause.]

I find that comforting, somehow. In the middle of the largest event in the century, there is a man with a lamp, doing a small job properly.

We do the same. Every night. Somebody, somewhere, is working on a small job, in the dark, and it matters to somebody, and it will never be in a book.

[Soft pause. Let the room tone settle.]

Now let us walk. Slowly. We will not wake anyone.

The streets at this hour belong to cats, and to the very old, and to the people who cannot sleep.

Here is an old man, sitting on a step, with a blanket over his shoulders. He is not doing anything. He is looking at the sky, which is still perfectly black. He has been there for some time.

He sees us. He nods. He does not say anything.

We nod back, and go on.

[Pause.]

Here is a lamp in a window.

Inside, there is a woman and a child. The child is asleep — deeply, in the way that children sleep, with one arm over the edge of the bed and their mouth open. The woman is awake, sitting on the floor beside the bed, with her back against the wall.

She is not crying. She is not praying. She is just sitting.

There is a bowl on the floor beside her with something in it that has gone cold.

She has been sitting there for a long time.

[Soft pause.]

Let us move on. This is not a thing to watch.

Down the hill, near the wall, there is a guard post, and there is a man in it, and the man is asleep.

Fully asleep. His helmet has slid down over his eyes. His spear is against the wall.

There is a rule about this, and the rule is a hard one, and if he is found he will be in serious trouble.

We are not going to find him.

[Pause. Very slight smile in the voice.]

The reason I tell you this is that in every account of every siege, the enemy is at the gate, and the men are brave, and the watch is kept. And in reality, at three in the morning, the watch is kept by a man who is asleep with his helmet over his eyes, and the city is still there, and nothing happens.

The world is held together by a great many people who are not doing their job particularly well, on any given night. It is somehow enough.

[Soft pause.]

Now let us go up onto the wall itself, and look out.

This is the part of the night I want you to remember.

From here, you can see both things at once — the city behind you, with its lamps, and its roofs, and its one open shutter; and the camp in front of you, with its fires, mostly banked down to orange, and its tents, and its shapes.

And between them, the ditch. The strip of bare earth we crossed earlier. It is completely empty.

[Pause.]

In a few hours, that strip of earth will be the most famous strip of earth in the world.

Tonight, it is just a ditch. There is grass in it. There is water at the bottom, very shallow, with the sky in it.

And it is quiet.

[Long pause. Ten seconds of nothing but room tone.]

Let us sit down for a while, here, on the parapet, with our backs against the stone.

This is the part of the story where I usually stop talking about events altogether, and I am going to do that now.

Because nothing is going to happen for a while. The gun is being repaired. The men are asleep. The emperor is asleep, or pretending to be. The ships are still. The chain is still up. The moon is going down.

And the city — the great, ancient, impossible city, with its thousand years and its five hundred thousand ghosts — is doing the only thing it can do at this hour.

It is breathing.

[Softly, slower now.]

In. And out.

In. And out.

If you are lying down while you listen to this — and I hope you are — you can do it too. Let your breathing match the city's. There is no hurry. There is nothing to do until morning.

In. And out.

[Long pause. Thirty seconds.]

When you are ready, we will go and watch the light come.

### X · THE FIRST LIGHT  [12:00 · ≈1,250 words]

[Distant birds. Water. A very slow swell in the music.]

Dawn in this part of the world does not arrive. It seeps.

First there is a change in the colour of the dark — not light, exactly, but a shift, as though the black had been mixed with a very small amount of grey. Then the water begins to show: a dull shine on the Golden Horn, a shine on the Bosphorus, a line of pale along the horizon that gets wider, very slowly, the way a bruise widens.

Then the shapes come back. The masts. The roofs. The wall.

And then, at some point that nobody can identify, it is morning.

[Pause.]

Let us be on the wall for this. It is the best place to be, and it is where the city is looking.

There are men all along the parapet now, standing, not sitting. Nobody has ordered them to stand. They have simply got up.

They are not talking. They are watching the east.

[Soft pause.]

The first bird goes up.

Then another. Then a dozen, in that loose, unbothered way that birds have, entirely uninterested in what is about to happen.

Somewhere below, in the city, a rooster.

And then, from the camp, the first sound of the day.

[Pause.]

It is not the gun. Not yet. It is the ordinary sound of an army waking up: a shout, a laugh, a hammer, the low mutter of a great many men who have slept on the ground and are not pleased about it.

And then the drums.

Not many. Not loud. Just a rhythm, somewhere out in the mist, that other drums begin to answer.

[Soft pause. Let the music tighten very slightly — then release.]

I am not going to describe the assault. That is not what this channel is for, and it is not what this night was.

What I will tell you is what it felt like from inside the city, in the first hour, in the way that people afterwards described it.

They described a noise.

Not a battle noise. A noise like the sea, if the sea were made of voices. It came over the wall in one long wave, and it did not stop, and it did not go down, and after a while the people in the streets stopped trying to hear individual words in it, because there were none.

And then, under the noise, there was something else.

[Pause.]

The wall was moving.

Not falling — moving. The stone under their feet was vibrating, very slightly, the way a floor vibrates when a heavy cart goes past outside. It had been doing this for weeks, in the daytime, when the gun fired.

But now it was doing it continuously.

[Soft pause.]

Here is the part I want you to hold on to, and then we will step away from the wall and go quietly into the city.

In the accounts that people wrote afterwards — the ones who were there, the ones who got out — almost nobody writes about the fighting. They write about small things.

A man writes about the smell of the dust. A woman writes about the fact that the birds had gone. Somebody writes about a neighbour who came out of his house with a loaf of bread in his hands and stood in the street, not knowing what to do with it, and then gave it to a child.

That is what people remember. Not the history. The bread.

[Pause.]

So let us do the same. Let us leave the wall, and go down into the streets, and be with the people who are not fighting, because they are the ones this log is for.

[Soft pause. Things getting quieter, not louder.]

The streets are full, and they are not in panic.

They are moving, and they are carrying things, and they are going towards the water. Some of them are going towards the churches. A few are going nowhere at all, standing in doorways with their hands at their sides.

And here is what is so strange, and so human, and so completely unlike the way we tell this story:

People are helping each other.

A man is carrying an old woman. A woman is holding two children by the hand and a bundle under her arm and is somehow also carrying a third child on her back. A boy is dragging a chest that is far too heavy for him, and a soldier stops, and picks up the other end, and they carry it together without a word.

Nobody is in charge of this. There is no plan. It is simply what people do, in the last hour, when there is nothing to be gained.

[Pause.]

Let us follow one group down to the water.

There is a small boat there, pulled up on the stones. It is not a good boat. It has been patched, and it leaks, and it was built for four people and there are nine in it.

The man holding the rope is not a sailor. You can tell by his hands.

He is waiting. He is not pushing off.

He is waiting because there is a woman running down the stones towards him, and she is carrying something, and he is not going to leave without her.

[Soft pause.]

She gets there. She climbs in. The boat goes out.

And behind it, on the wall, the smoke is rising, and the noise is still going, and the sun is fully up now, and the water is doing what water does — it closes over, and becomes flat, and reflects the sky.

[Long pause. Let the music be very quiet.]

I am not going to tell you what happened next. You know what happened next. It is in the books, and it is in the marble, and it is in the name of the city.

What I want to tell you is what happened after. Not the conquest. The other thing.

The thing that was saved.

[Soft pause.]

### XI · WHAT WAS SAVED  [13:00 · ≈1,450 words]

[Paper. A page turning, very close. A quill, once.]

Let us go back to the garden.

The one at the end of the narrow lane, near the southern wall, with the small building full of paper.

It is morning now, and the light is coming in through a window that has no glass in it, and the garden is completely quiet, and there are three people in the room with the books.

They have been here all night.

[Pause.]

We left them choosing. Now they are finished choosing.

There is a chest on the floor, and it is small — that is the first thing you notice, and it is the thing that makes the scene what it is. It is a chest that one man can carry. Not two. One, on his own, at a run, if he has to.

And it is full.

[Soft pause.]

Now let me tell you what is in chests like this one. Not this chest — this one we cannot know — but chests like it, because we do know what came out of this city in those days, and where it went, and what it became.

There were manuscripts.

Not printed books — printing had not come to this part of the world yet. Manuscripts: written by hand, on parchment, in Greek, copied by monks and scholars over centuries, one letter at a time, in cold rooms, with bad light.

Aristotle. Plato. Ptolemy. Galen. Euclid. Herodotus. Thucydides. The mathematicians, the physicians, the astronomers, the historians.

And the ones nobody remembers: the commentaries, the marginal notes, the letters, the lists, the medical recipes, the grammars. The working papers of a civilisation, which are always more interesting than its monuments.

[Pause.]

Here is what happened to them.

Some were carried to Italy by people who had booked passage on the boats and paid for it with everything they owned. Some were carried by merchants who did not care about the books at all, and simply took them because they were worth something, and sold them in Venice. Some were left behind, and survived anyway, in the houses of people who stayed, and were found later, in attics, in the next century.

And some were lost.

We do not know how many. That is the honest answer. The libraries of this city were among the greatest in the world, and we have a list of what was in them, and the list is much longer than the things we still have.

[Soft pause.]

But here is the thing that matters, and it is the reason I wanted to end this log here.

When those manuscripts arrived in Italy, something happened that nobody had planned.

There were already scholars in Florence and Venice and Rome who could read Greek — a few, a handful, mostly people who had learned it from Byzantines who had come west earlier. And there were already printers, though not many, and not yet very good.

The two things met.

[Pause.]

Within fifty years of this night, the Greek texts that had been copied by hand in Constantinople for a thousand years were being printed in Venice by the thousand. Not by the dozen. By the thousand.

Aristotle, printed. Plato, printed. The Greek mathematicians, printed. The medicine of Galen, printed. The geography of Ptolemy, printed — with maps, which changed what people thought the world looked like.

And the men who made this possible were, very often, the men who had left this city in the weeks before it fell, carrying a chest.

[Soft pause.]

I want to be careful not to oversimplify. History is not a relay race, and the Renaissance was not caused by one night, or one chest, or one city. It had a hundred causes, and most of them have nothing to do with Constantinople.

But this much is true, and it is enough:

The people in that garden did not save the city. Nobody could have saved the city.

They saved the pages.

[Long pause. Let it sit. This is the emotional centre of the episode.]

And a page is a strange thing to save, when you think about it. It has no military value. It will not feed anyone. If you are running for a boat, a page is the least useful object in the room.

But it is also the only object in the room that can be copied.

You cannot copy a church. You cannot copy a wall, or a harbour, or an empire. But a page — a page you can copy. And then it is two pages, and then it is twenty, and then it is in a book, and then it is in a thousand books, and then it is in a schoolroom in a country that did not exist yet, being read by a child whose grandparents had never heard of Constantinople.

[Pause.]

That is the whole reason this channel is called what it is called.

Vellum. The skin of an animal, scraped thin, and stretched, and dried, and used for writing.

It is not a grand word. It is not a monument. It is the material of a page.

But it is the thing that survives.

[Soft pause. The quill again.]

Let us leave the garden now. The three people in it have finished. They are tying the chest with a rope, and one of them is crying, and the other two are letting him, and outside, the noise is getting closer.

They will get out. Some of them. Not all.

We do not need to follow them to the boat. We know how this goes. What we need is to understand what they were doing, and then to let them go.

They were doing what people do when everything else has failed. They were choosing the smallest thing worth saving, and carrying it.

[Long pause.]

### XII · WHAT THE LOG REMEMBERS  [13:00 · ≈1,200 words]

[Very soft. Almost only room tone and a distant bell.]

It has been five hundred and seventy-three years.

The walls are still there, in places. So is the great church, though it is not a church any more; it is a mosque, and then it was a museum, and now it is a mosque again. The city has a different name. The empire is gone. The language that was spoken in those streets is spoken now in a few thousand houses, and in a few universities, and in the services of a small church in a side street in a city that is no longer called what it was called.

And yet we are still telling this story.

[Pause.]

Why?

I have thought about this a great deal, and I want to offer you three answers, and then I want to stop talking and let you sleep.

[Soft pause.]

The first answer is that it is a good story. That is not a shallow thing to say. A good story is a real thing, and this one has all the parts: a city, a wall, a gun, a young man with an impossible plan, an old man who would not leave. It has a beginning, a middle, and an end. It even has a moral, which is rare.

But a good story is not enough. There are a thousand good stories, and we do not tell them.

[Pause.]

The second answer is that it is the end of something we can still feel. The Roman Empire lasted for two thousand two hundred years, if you count from the founding of the Republic, and more than a thousand if you count from Constantine. It is the longest-lived political thing in the western world. Everything that came after — the law, the language, the calendar, the shape of the cities, the idea of a senate, the idea that a citizen has rights — is downstream of it.

And it ended on a Tuesday morning, in a city of nine thousand people, at the hands of an army that was younger than the wall it was attacking.

There is something in that which we cannot quite get our heads around. The largest thing we know, ending in a single morning.

[Soft pause.]

The third answer is the one I actually believe.

We keep telling this story because all of us have had a last night.

[Pause. Let it land. Slow down here.]

Not a siege. Not a city. Something smaller.

A room you left for the last time and did not know it was the last time. A person you said goodbye to in an ordinary way, at an ordinary hour, thinking you would see them again. A job. A house. A version of yourself that you were, for a while, and then were not.

We do not get to choose which nights are the last ones. That is the whole difficulty. If we knew, we would pay attention. We would notice the dog in the road, and the cup tied to the fountain, and the sound of the bell over the water.

But we do not know. So we miss them. And then, years later, we find ourselves thinking about a bakery, or a street, or the way the light came in through a window, and we understand that we were there for the last time.

[Soft pause.]

The people in this story did know.

That is the only thing that is different about them, and it is everything. They knew. And so they did what people do when they know: they went to the service. They mended the net. They baked the bread. They carried the chest. They sat with their backs against the stone, and they looked at the lights of their own city, and they let it be what it was.

That is not heroism. It is something better, and much more ordinary. It is attention.

[Long pause. Very slow now.]

So here is what I would like you to take from this log, if you take anything.

The next time you are somewhere ordinary — a kitchen, a street, a room with a lamp in it and somebody breathing in the next room — try to notice that you are there.

Not in a sad way. Not as though it is the last time. Just notice.

You do not need a siege to make an evening matter. You only need to be awake for it.

[Pause.]

And now — because it is late, and because that is what this channel is for — it is time to stop being awake.

[Soft pause.]

### XIII · SLEEP  [8:00 · ≈350 words]

[The room tone drops. The bell, once, very far away. Then only the ambient bed.]

Let us close the log.

We are back at the window with the open shutter, and the city is below us, and the lamps are going out one by one, the way they always do at the end of a night.

There is nothing more to decide. There is nothing more to prepare.

The wall is where it is. The water is where it is. The boy with the candle has gone home, or has fallen asleep where he stood. The man on the step has gone inside. The guard is still asleep with his helmet over his eyes, and the city is still there, and it is quiet.

[Soft pause.]

If you have stayed with me all this way, thank you. That is not a small thing — to give a night to a city that is not yours, and to a story that ended before your grandparents were born.

But you did, and now it is over, and there is nothing left to do but rest.

[Pause.]

The log is closed now.

The shelf is dark.

The lamp is low.

Sleep well.

[Silence. Sixty seconds of ambient bed only. Then fade to black.]
