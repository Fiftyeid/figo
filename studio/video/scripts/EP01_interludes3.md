### F · THE MARBLE EMPEROR  [≈12:00 · ≈1,550 words]

[Wind in a high place. Stone room tone. Very low bell, far off.]

There is a gate in this city that has not been opened in a hundred and fifty years.

[Pause.]

It is not the gate we have been looking at. That one is in the wall we have walked along, the great land wall, the one with the emperor's name on it, and it is very busy tonight, because gates are always busy when a city is frightened.

This other gate is in a different wall, on the other side of the city, facing the land road that comes from the west. It is tall, and it is white, and it is made of marble, and it has been bricked up.

[Soft pause.]

Not damaged. Not fallen. Bricked up. Deliberately, with courses of brick laid across the arch, the way you would close a window in a house you were not planning to leave.

And above the arch, there is an inscription, and the inscription says, more or less: this gate was made in the reign of an emperor whose name everyone has now forgotten, for the glory of the city, and for the entry of those who come in peace.

[Pause.]

I want to tell you why it is closed, because the reason is not military. The reason is a story.

[Soft pause.]

The story goes like this.

There was an emperor — and there have been many emperors, and in a story like this it does not matter which one — who was a great soldier, and who went out from this city to fight, and who did not come back.

And on the day he left, or the day he died, or the day the city decided he had died, which is not the same thing, a legend began to grow around him, the way a pearl grows around a piece of grit.

The legend said that he was not dead.

It said that he had been taken by an angel, and turned to marble, and laid in a cave beneath this gate, and that he would sleep there until the city needed him.

[Pause.]

And then — this is the part that everybody loves, and I love it too — and then he would wake, and the marble would crack, and he would stand up, and he would take his sword, and he would go out through this gate, and he would drive the enemy from the walls.

[Soft pause.]

That is the story. It is not a good story. It does not have to be. It is a thing that people told each other in the dark, and it did its job, which was to make the dark a little less dark.

[Pause.]

Now. Here is what I find interesting.

The gate is bricked up.

Not because the legend was believed. Because the legend was embarrassing.

[Soft pause.]

There is a version of this in every city, and I want to say it plainly, because it is one of the strangest and most human things about us.

A people will build a gate for an emperor. Then they will watch the emperor die. Then they will invent a story in which he did not die. Then they will brick up the gate, because the story is now too much for them, and because a gate through which a dead man is expected to return is not a gate. It is a wound.

[Pause.]

I do not know which emperor it was. Sources disagree, and the sources that agree are probably wrong, and it does not matter.

What matters is that on the night of the twenty-eighth of May, there are people in this city who believe it.

[Soft pause.]

Not many. Not the emperor. Not the patriarch. Not the men on the wall, who are practical in the way that men on walls always are.

But somewhere in this city, in a house with a low door, there is an old woman who was told this story when she was a child, and who has told it to her grandchildren, and who is telling it again tonight, in a voice that is trying to be calm.

And there is a boy listening to her. And he is not thinking about the wall, or the guns, or the ships. He is thinking about a man made of marble, asleep under a gate, waiting.

[Pause.]

I want to be fair to that boy. It would be easy, from here, from six hundred years away, to call the story superstition, and to smile at it, and to move on.

But let me ask you something.

[Soft pause.]

What else was he supposed to think about?

[Pause.]

He is twelve, or eleven, or nine. He cannot fight. He cannot vote. He cannot advise the emperor. He has been told, by every adult he trusts, that something terrible is going to happen, and that nobody can stop it.

And then somebody tells him a story about a marble emperor who wakes when the city needs him.

That story is not a lie. It is a tool. It is the only tool that a child in that situation has been given, and it is a good tool, and it does the one thing that a tool is supposed to do, which is to let him get through the night.

[Pause.]

Now let me tell you the rest, because there is a rest.

[Soft pause.]

The city falls. The gate stays closed. The marble emperor does not wake.

And the story does not die.

[Pause.]

It changes. It moves. It gets told in other cities, by other people, in other languages, and it becomes something else — not a hope for this city, but a hope for all of them. A story about a kingdom that is not finished. A story about a king who is only sleeping.

And in the centuries that follow, when people look at this city and see a place that used to be the centre of the world, they will remember the marble emperor, and they will say: he is still down there.

[Soft pause.]

I do not think he is down there. I think he is a story, and I think the gate is bricked up, and I think the men who bricked it up were right to do it.

But I also think that a people who can build a story like that, and carry it for a thousand years, and hand it to their children on the worst night of their lives — I think that is a people who are not finished.

[Pause.]

Let us walk to the gate.

It takes a while. The city is dark, and the streets on this side are not the streets we know. There is a long straight road, and a column, and a place where the ground rises, and then the gate, white in the dark, with the brick courses across the arch like a bandage.

There is nobody here. That is the strangest thing about it. In a city that is full of men on walls and women in churches and children in doorways, this place is empty.

[Soft pause.]

Stand here for a moment. Look at the brick.

Somebody laid those courses. Somebody mixed the mortar and carried the bricks up a ladder and set them, one by one, across an arch that had been open for a thousand years, and when he was finished he climbed down and looked at his work, and went home.

I wonder what he thought.

[Pause.]

Did he think: this is the end of something? Did he think: this is a stupid job and I am being paid badly? Did he think about the emperor under the gate, and feel a small, unreasonable hope that the bricks would fall down in the night?

I do not know. He did not write it down. He is a man with a trowel, and men with trowels do not write things down.

But the bricks are still there. And that is a kind of testimony.

[Soft pause.]

Tonight, on the other side of the city, the real gate is about to be tested. And here, on this side, the gate that nobody will test is closed, and quiet, and waiting for a man made of marble who is not going to come.

[Pause.]

I find that unbearably sad, and I find it beautiful, and I cannot separate the two, and I do not think I am supposed to.

[Long pause. Wind.]

Before we go back, do one thing for me.

Put your hand on the brick.

[Pause.]

It is cold. It is always cold at this hour, even in summer. And under the brick, there is the arch, and under the arch, there is the dark of the gateway, and under the gateway, there is the ground, and under the ground, there is nothing at all.

[Soft pause.]

The emperor is not there. He never was. He is a story that a city told itself when it was frightened, and the story has outlived the city, and that is the only kind of immortality that anyone in this night is going to get.

[Pause.]

And now let us go back.

The wall is waiting. The lamps are still lit. And somewhere, in a house with a low door, a boy is falling asleep with a marble emperor in his head.

[Long pause. Wind fades.]

### G · THE WITNESS  [≈12:00 · ≈1,550 words]

[Water close. A pen. A page. Very low room tone.]

There is a man in this city who is not from this city, and who is going to write all of this down.

[Pause.]

Let us go and find him.

He is not on the wall. He is not in the palace. He is not in a church, and he is not in the camp outside, and he is not on the quay with the boats.

He is on a ship.

[Soft pause.]

The ship is in the Golden Horn, inside the chain, and it is not a warship, and it is not a merchantman. It is a ship from one of the Italian cities — Venice, in this case, though there are Genoese here too, and the two groups have been arguing about precedence for so long that they have almost forgotten what the argument was about.

[Pause.]

The man's name is Nicolò. He is a surgeon. He is on this ship because ships need surgeons, and because the Venetian community here is a small, stubborn, businesslike colony that has decided to stay, and because — this is the part that matters — somebody has to be here.

He is not a hero. Let me say that clearly, because he will be remembered as one, and he would have found that funny.

He is a man with a notebook.

[Soft pause.]

Let me describe him, as best we can, because there is no portrait, and the descriptions that survive are all of a man with a notebook.

He is in his thirties. He has cold hands — everybody has cold hands at three in the morning, even in May — and he is writing by the light of a lamp that he has set on a coil of rope so that it does not move with the ship.

The ink is in a small horn. The pen is a reed. The notebook is paper, not parchment, because paper is cheaper and because this is not a book, this is a record.

[Pause.]

And what does a man write, on a night like this?

[Soft pause.]

Not what you would expect.

He does not write: the city is doomed. He does not write: we are all going to die. He does not write about glory, or God, or the end of the world.

He writes the schedule.

[Pause.]

He writes that on the twenty-eighth of May, there was firing from the great gun in the morning, and that the wall near the gate of Saint Romanus was damaged again, and that the damage was repaired in the night. He writes that a ship came in, or did not come in. He writes that a man was wounded in the arm by a stone from the wall, and that he, Nicolò, dressed the wound, and that the man would live.

He writes the number of ships. He writes the direction of the wind.

[Soft pause.]

And every so often, in the middle of a list of guns and dates and wind directions, he writes a sentence that has nothing to do with any of it.

[Pause.]

A sentence about the noise. A sentence about how the sound of the great gun could be heard in the houses across the water, and how the windows shook. A sentence about a woman who came to the ship asking for news of her husband, and would not leave, and had to be led away.

A sentence about how quiet the city was at the second hour of the night.

[Soft pause.]

That is what a witness is. Not a man who understands. A man who notices.

[Pause.]

I want you to notice something about him, because it is the reason I have brought you here.

He is writing this by hand, in a notebook, on a ship, in the middle of the night, in a city that may fall before morning.

Why?

[Soft pause.]

Not for us. He does not know that we exist. He cannot imagine a world in which a person, six hundred years later, would sit in a dark room and listen to a voice reading his notebook aloud.

He is writing for three reasons, and I think all three of them are true at once.

[Pause.]

The first is that he is a professional, and professionals keep records. A surgeon writes down what he treated and how it went, because that is how you get better, and because that is how you get paid.

The second is that he is angry, and frightened, and writing is what he does with it. A man who is angry and frightened and has a pen will use the pen. It is that simple.

And the third — and this is the one I want to give him — the third is that he believes, quietly, without saying so, that it matters what happened.

[Soft pause.]

Not what people will say happened. Not the version that will be told in the churches and the courts. What actually happened.

The wind. The number of ships. The man with the wound in his arm.

[Pause.]

That belief is not a small thing. It is, in fact, the thing that this whole night is going to be built on, in the centuries to come. Because when the city falls — and it does fall — there will be a great many stories told about it, and some of them will be true, and some of them will be propaganda, and some of them will be the kind of story that a defeated people tells itself so that it can go on being a people.

And in the middle of all of them, there will be a notebook.

[Soft pause.]

Written by a man with cold hands, on a ship, by the light of a lamp on a coil of rope.

[Pause.]

Let us look over his shoulder for a moment. Not to read the words — they are in a language you may not know, and the handwriting is small and hurried, and the lamp is not good.

Just to see the page.

[Soft pause.]

It is not a beautiful page. There are crossings-out. There are lines that run downhill, because the ship moved. There is a blot where the pen caught.

And at the bottom of the page, in the last line, the hand changes. It gets smaller, and slightly faster, and you can see the moment when he stopped writing for other people and started writing for himself.

[Pause.]

That is the line I would keep.

[Soft pause.]

Now. The lamp is low. The ship is quiet. Somewhere across the water, a gun is being loaded in the dark, and the men are talking in low voices, and the rope is creaking, and the city is asleep in the way that a city sleeps when it knows it is being watched.

And the surgeon closes the notebook, and puts the pen in the horn, and sits for a moment with his hands on his knees.

[Pause.]

He does not know how it ends. That is the thing that separates him from us, and it is the thing I envy him for, and the thing I would not wish on anyone.

[Soft pause.]

He does not know that the notebook will survive. He does not know that it will be copied, and printed, and translated, and that schoolchildren in countries that have not yet been founded will read a paragraph of it and be bored.

He does not know that his name will be on a title page, and that the title page will be in a library, and that the library will be in a city he has never heard of.

[Pause.]

All he knows is that it is the twenty-eighth of May, and the wind is from the north, and the wall near the gate of Saint Romanus is damaged again, and he has a notebook, and the lamp is still burning.

[Soft pause.]

That is enough. That is what a witness is for.

[Long pause.]

Let us leave him. He has work in the morning, and so do we.

[Pause. Page turns. Water fades.]
