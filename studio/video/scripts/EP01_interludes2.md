### D · THE DOGS OF THE CITY  [≈12:00 · ≈1,550 words]

[Very low room tone. Somewhere far off, one dog barks twice and stops.]

There is one part of this city that has not been told that there is a siege.

It is asleep.

[Pause.]

Let us leave the wall for a while. Let us leave the guns, and the emperor, and the men with the lamps. It is still the same night, and there is still time, and I want to show you something small.

Come down off the wall. Turn left at the bottom of the stair. Walk past the church with the cracked step, and the house with the fig tree, and the place where the street gets so narrow that two men cannot pass without turning sideways.

And then stop, and listen.

[Pause. Night.]

There. Under the eaves of the baker's shop, where the warm air comes out of the oven all night and makes a small pocket of summer in the cold.

There is a dog.

[Soft pause.]

Not a pet. Nobody in this city has a pet in the way you and I mean the word. She belongs to the street, which is to say she belongs to nobody and is fed by everybody, and she has been fed by this street for six years, and she knows every doorway on it better than the men who live in them.

Tonight she is lying with her back against the warm wall of the oven, and her paws are twitching, and she is dreaming.

[Pause.]

I want to say something about that, and I want to say it without making it into a lesson, because it is not a lesson. It is just a fact, and it is one of the truest facts about this whole night.

The guns have been firing for weeks. The wall has been shaking. The men on the wall have not slept properly in a month.

And the dog is asleep.

[Pause.]

She is not brave. She is not indifferent. She has simply done what every animal does, which is to live in the world as it actually is, rather than in the world as it might become.

When there is food, she eats. When there is danger, she runs. When there is neither, she sleeps, because sleep is free and tomorrow is not guaranteed and the wall is warm.

[Soft pause.]

There are, in this city, a great many animals.

There are dogs, in every street, in every colour that a dog can be, mostly the colour of dust. There are cats, which in this city are not the sleek cats of the paintings but thin, serious, competent cats who live in the roofs and the cisterns and the spaces between buildings, and who are, if we are honest, better at sieges than the people.

There are horses — not many now, because the horses were eaten or requisitioned or both. There are mules. There is a bear, in a courtyard near the harbour, that a man keeps for reasons that are not clear to anyone including the man. There are pigeons, which nobody thinks about and which will still be here when all of us are gone. There are bees, in the garden of a monastery, in three hives, doing the only work in this city that the siege cannot interrupt.

[Pause.]

And there are the rats.

I am not going to spend long on the rats, because they are not romantic and because you already know what they mean. But I want to mention them, because a city at war is not only a city of soldiers. It is an ecosystem, and an ecosystem under stress does strange things, and the men who run this city know it, and the word they use for it is not a pretty word.

[Soft pause.]

Let us go back to the dog.

She wakes up. Not because of a noise — because of a smell. Somebody is coming down the street with a basket, and the basket has bread in it, and the bread is not for her, but a dog who has lived six years in this street knows that a man carrying bread will drop something, or will be persuaded to.

She stands. She stretches, front legs first, then back, in the long luxurious way that dogs stretch when they have been asleep against a warm wall.

And then she does something I want you to notice.

She does not run to the man. She walks. She walks at an angle, slightly away from him, and then slightly toward, in the way that a dog walks when she is not sure of her welcome and does not want to seem desperate.

[Pause.]

That walk is the whole of life in this city tonight. Everybody is walking at an angle. Nobody wants to seem desperate. Everybody wants bread.

[Soft pause.]

The man with the basket is a deacon. He is taking bread to the church, for the liturgy that is happening later, and he has a great deal on his mind, and he does not see the dog at all.

She follows him for a while. Not close. Twenty paces. Then ten. Then five.

And at the corner, where the street opens into the little square with the plane tree, she stops, and sits down, and watches him go.

[Pause.]

There is another dog in the square. A bigger one, older, with a torn ear. He is lying across the doorway of a closed shop, and he lifts his head as the deacon passes, and then puts it down again.

The two dogs look at each other. That is all. They do not growl. They do not fight. There is a whole conversation in it, and the conversation is about territory and seniority and the fact that neither of them has eaten since yesterday.

Then the older one closes his eyes, and the younger one lies down in the dust of the square, and the night goes on.

[Soft pause.]

I know what you might be thinking. This is a story about a siege, and I am telling you about dogs.

But I would ask you to stay with me for one more minute, because there is something here that I have not seen said well anywhere else.

[Pause.]

When we tell the story of a city's last night, we tell it in the language of decisions. The emperor decides. The sultan decides. The general decides. The council decides.

But most of what happens in a city is not decided. It is continued.

The dog continues. The bees continue. The cat on the roof continues. The man with the bread continues, because the bread has to be baked and carried and blessed, and if he stops to think about the wall he will not carry it.

[Pause.]

That is not stupidity. It is the deepest form of courage there is, and it does not look like courage at all. It looks like a man carrying a basket.

[Soft pause.]

Let me tell you one more thing about the animals, and then we will go back to the people, because the people are waiting.

In the last weeks, the price of feed has gone up, and the price of meat has gone up, and there are horses in this city that have been turned loose because nobody can feed them.

They are still here. You can see them at night, in the open ground near the cisterns, standing in the dark with their heads down. They do not run away. There is nowhere to run to. They stand where they were left, and they wait, in the way that horses wait, which is without complaint and without hope and without any apparent sense that anything is wrong.

[Pause.]

Tomorrow, some of them will be eaten. Some of them will be taken. Some of them will simply be found, weeks later, standing in the same place.

I do not say this to make you sad. I say it because it is part of the night, and because a night that leaves out the animals is not a true night.

[Soft pause.]

Now. Back to the square. Back past the plane tree. Back up the street toward the wall.

The dog with the torn ear lifts his head one more time as we pass, and watches us go with the calm, level, entirely unsentimental gaze of an animal that has decided we are not a threat and not a meal.

And behind us, in the bakery doorway, the warm air keeps coming out, and the other dog comes back, and turns around three times, and lies down.

[Pause.]

She will be asleep when the first light comes. She will be asleep when the boats come. She will be asleep, probably, when the city falls, because a dog in a bakery doorway does not know what a city is.

And in the morning, when the streets are full of men she has never seen, she will do the only thing she knows how to do, which is to walk at an angle, slightly away and then slightly toward, and wait to see what happens.

[Long pause. Distant dog, once.]

That is the last we will see of her. I am glad she got the warm wall.

[Pause. Tone fades.]

### E · THE ROAD OUT  [≈12:00 · ≈1,550 words]

[Water against a hull. Rope, slow. No wind.]

There is a harbour in this city, and it is not the one we have been looking at.

The Golden Horn is the famous water. It is the water of the chain, and the ships, and the dragging of seventy hulls over a hill. It is the water that everyone will remember.

But there is another harbour, on the other side, on the sea of Marmara, and it is smaller and quieter and it faces a different direction, and from it, on a night like this, a few boats leave.

[Pause.]

Let us go and stand on the quay.

It is not a busy quay. There is no market here, no shouting, no ropes being thrown. There are three boats, and a man with a lantern, and a group of people standing very close together in the dark, the way people stand when they are cold and also when they are afraid.

They are not soldiers. Look at them.

There is a woman with a bundle. There is an old man with a box on his knees, sitting on the stone, holding it the way you hold a child. There is a boy of about twelve who is not holding anything at all, and who keeps looking back at the city, and then away, and then back.

And there is a monk with two satchels and a face like a man who has been awake for three days.

[Pause.]

I want to be careful here, because there is a version of this scene that is easy to tell and hard to forgive. The version where the brave people stay and the frightened people leave.

That is not what is happening.

[Soft pause.]

What is happening is that a decision was made, weeks ago, in a room with a low ceiling and too many people in it, and the decision was this: some things must not be in the city when the city falls.

Not people. Things.

[Pause.]

Let me explain. This city is not only a place. It is a library, and a workshop, and a school, and a treasury, and a reliquary, and all of those things are made of objects, and the objects can be carried.

A manuscript can be carried. A chalice can be carried. A relic in a silver box can be carried. A copy of a book that exists nowhere else in the world can be carried, if you are willing to be the person who carries it.

A mosaic cannot be carried. A church cannot. A wall cannot.

[Soft pause.]

So the decision was: save what can be carried. And then find the people who are willing to carry it, and give them a boat, and a letter, and a destination, and very little else.

[Pause.]

The monk is one of those people. The two satchels are not his. They belong to the monastery, and they contain, at a guess, four books and a box of loose quires that were not yet bound.

Four books.

I want you to hold that number for a moment. Four books, in two satchels, on a boat, at night, in the last week of May.

If the boat sinks, four books are gone. Not copies. Not editions. The things themselves.

[Pause.]

The woman with the bundle is carrying something different. Her bundle is cloth, and inside the cloth there is a small icon, and the icon is not valuable, and it is not old, and it is not beautiful, and it has been in her family for four generations, and she would rather leave her shoes than leave it.

The old man with the box is carrying a box of seeds. Vegetable seeds. Beans, and something leafy, and a kind of small onion that his family has grown in the same garden for as long as anyone can remember.

[Soft pause.]

That is the part of this scene that I find hardest, and I have thought about it for a long time.

The monk is saving civilisation. We can all agree about that. It is a noble thing to carry four books out of a falling city.

But the old man is saving a garden. And in a hundred years, in a place far from here, somebody will be eating that small onion, and they will not know where it came from, and they will call it by a name that has nothing to do with this city.

And that is also civilisation. It is just a slower, quieter kind, and it does not get a chapter in the histories.

[Pause.]

Let us listen to the harbour for a moment.

[Pause. Water.]

The water is doing what water does at a quay: slapping, softly, in a rhythm that is almost but not quite regular. A rope is creaking. Somebody is crying, very quietly, in the way that people cry when they do not want to be heard and have not yet decided whether to stop.

The man with the lantern is not crying. He is counting. He is counting the people, and the bundles, and the space in the boat, and he is doing it with the expression of a man who has been given a job that is too big for him and has decided to do it anyway.

[Pause.]

Here is a thing about leaving that nobody tells you.

The people on the quay are not leaving because they are afraid. They are leaving because they were asked, and because they said yes, and because saying yes meant saying it in front of other people who had said no.

The ones who stay will remember them. That is the cost. Not the sea, not the journey, not the unknown country. The cost is that the people who stay will remember them as the ones who left.

[Soft pause.]

And they know it.

The monk knows it. He has a brother in the monastery who is staying, and they did not say goodbye properly, because a proper goodbye would have been a statement, and neither of them wanted to make a statement.

They said: I will see you.

Which is what people say when they know they will not.

[Pause.]

Now. The boat is ready.

The lantern goes to the bow. The rope comes off the post, and is coiled, and thrown. The sail is not raised yet, because there is no wind in the harbour, and because a sail is visible, and because the first part of this journey is done with oars.

The people get in. They do it slowly, because the boat is low and the stone is wet, and because everybody is carrying something, and because nobody wants to be the first to sit down.

And then the oars go out, and the boat moves, and the gap between the boat and the quay gets bigger.

[Pause.]

The boy who kept looking back at the city is looking back at it now.

He is not looking at the wall. The wall is behind the houses from here. He is looking at the roofs, and the domes, and the one tall column that you can see from the water, and the small yellow lights in the windows.

He is trying to remember it. That is what his face is doing. He is trying to fix it, all of it, in a place inside himself where it will still be there in forty years.

[Soft pause.]

He will not manage it. Nobody does. In forty years he will remember the colour of the water and the smell of the harbour and one particular dog, and he will not be able to remember the shape of the domes, and it will hurt him more than he expects.

But he will remember that he tried.

[Pause.]

The boat goes out past the sea wall. The oars are quiet. There is a lantern at the stern now, and it gets smaller.

And then there is a moment — you have seen it, if you have ever watched a boat leave at night — when the lantern is just a point, and then it is a suggestion of a point, and then it is nothing at all, and the sea is the same colour as the sky.

[Long pause. Water.]

The quay is empty. The man with the lantern has gone back up the steps. There is a coil of rope on the stone that nobody picked up.

And the city is exactly as it was, except that four books are not in it, and a box of seeds is not in it, and a boy who was going to be a baker is not in it.

[Pause.]

We are not going to follow the boat. This is a night in one city, and the boat is going somewhere else.

But I want to leave you with a fact, and then we will go back to the wall.

[Soft pause.]

Most of the books that survive from this city survive because somebody carried them out. Not because they were hidden. Not because they were lucky. Because a person, on a night like this one, put them in a bag, and got on a boat, and sat in the dark for three days, and did not put the bag down.

[Pause.]

That is what a civilisation is, in the end.

A bag that somebody would not put down.

[Long pause. Water fades.]
