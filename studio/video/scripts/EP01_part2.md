# THE LAST NIGHT OF CONSTANTINOPLE — PART II
## Chapters IV – VII (verbatim narration)

---

### IV · THE SHIPS IN THE HORN  [13:00 · ≈1,450 words]

[Water against wood. Rope creaking, very slow.]

Let us leave the camp now, and go back across the ditch, and around the walls, to the water on the northern side.

This is the Golden Horn. A long, curved inlet of the sea that runs beside the city like a river somebody forgot to finish. It is deep, and it is sheltered, and it is the reason this city was built here and not somewhere else.

On a calm night, the Horn is almost black. The water holds no light of its own. It only holds what is given to it — the lamps along the walls, the low moon, the small orange glow of a single boat.

And across the mouth of this inlet, there is a chain.

[Pause.]

You would not think, from the sound of the word, that a chain could matter so much. But this one does. It is not a small thing — it is made of great iron links, and it is supported on wooden floats, so that it lies across the water like a line drawn by someone very patient. One end is fixed to a tower on the city walls. The other end is fixed to a tower on the far shore.

When the chain is up, nothing comes in. When the chain is up, the city has a harbour that cannot be reached.

And a harbour that cannot be reached is not a small advantage in a siege.

[Pause. Water.]

Let us be clear about why.

A city can be defended from the land. That is what walls are for. Walls are a promise: we will make you pay for every step.

But a city cannot be fed from the land. Not a city of this size, not with an army sitting on the fields outside. Bread does not grow on walls. Fish does not swim in a ditch.

Everything that keeps a city alive comes by water. Grain from the Black Sea. Wine. Oil. Dried fish. Rope. Nails. Charcoal. Letters. People.

So the defenders' whole hope rests on two things: that the wall holds, and that the water stays open.

That is why the chain is there. That is why it is guarded. That is why, every evening, men walk along the floats with a lantern, checking the links, checking the ropes, checking that nothing has slipped.

It is the least dramatic job in the city, and it is the one that matters most.

[Soft pause.]

Now. Let us look at the water inside the chain.

There are ships here. Not many. Maybe twenty-six, of different sizes, some of them merchant vessels that came in before the siege and never left. Their sails are furled. Their decks are quiet. There are men asleep under the thwarts, and men on watch, and a lamp at the masthead of one ship that swings very slightly with the water.

This is the last fleet of Constantinople. It is not a war fleet. It is a collection of whatever could float and be useful.

And in the darkness beyond the chain, out on the open water of the Bosphorus, there are other ships. Many more. They are not moving. They are waiting.

They have been waiting for weeks.

[Pause.]

Here is the thing that nobody in the city can explain, and that the men on the wall talk about in low voices.

The enemy has a fleet. The enemy has more men than the city has people. The enemy has money, and wood, and rope, and time. But the enemy cannot get past a chain.

They have tried. They have rowed up to it in the dark, and been driven back by the ships inside and the archers on the walls. They have tried to burn it. They have tried to cut it. Each time, the chain has held.

So for weeks, this has been the shape of the siege: an army that cannot get in by land, and a fleet that cannot get in by water.

A stalemate made of stone and iron.

[Long pause. Only water.]

Now let me tell you about the night the shape changed. And I want to tell it slowly, because the strangest things in history are usually the quietest.

It happened in the last week of April. Not with a battle. Not with a bombardment. With a decision.

The decision was this: if the ships cannot go over the chain, then the ships will go around it.

Around it. Over the hill behind the far shore. Over land.

[Pause.]

I know how that sounds. It sounds like something a child would suggest, and an adult would laugh at. Ships are heavy. Hills are steep. And the far shore is not ours — it belongs to a Genoese colony called Galata, which is officially neutral and unofficially helpful to whoever is winning.

But the decision was made. And then, one night, the work began.

[Soft pause.]

Let us go and watch it.

It is very late. There is no moon worth speaking of. On the slope behind Galata, between the cypress trees and the low walls of the vineyards, there is a line of men. Hundreds of them. They are not marching. They are crouched, and pulling, and sweating, and saying nothing.

Between them and the water, there is a road.

But it is not a road. It is a path made of planks, laid end to end, greased with fat and oil, so that a heavy thing can slide where it could never roll. From the shore, up the hill, over the crest, and down the other side into the Horn.

And on those planks, moving very slowly, there are ships.

[Pause.]

Imagine the size of them. Not the great ships — the smaller ones, the ones that can be dragged. But even a small ship is thirty paces long, and wide as a house, and made of oak, and heavy with its own keel.

Imagine the ropes. Imagine the men leaning into them, shoulder to shoulder, feet slipping on the greased wood. Imagine the sound: not shouting — they cannot shout, they must not be heard — but breathing. A hundred men breathing together. And under the breathing, the long, low groan of oak moving over fat.

Imagine the oxen, and the men who walk beside the hull with wedges, pushing them under, taking them out, pushing them under again.

And imagine the night, all around it. The dark. The smell of pine tar and sweat and crushed grass. The lights of the city across the water, so close that a man could throw a stone at them, and so far that nothing can be done.

[Long pause.]

They did this for two nights.

Nobody in the city noticed. That is the part I want you to sit with. For two nights, seventy ships were dragged over a hill, within sight of the walls of Constantinople, and the people inside — the guards on the wall, the fishermen on the water, the men checking the chain — noticed nothing.

Because it was quiet. Because it was slow. Because the most enormous thing in the world was happening a few hundred paces away, and it sounded like breathing.

[Soft pause. Water.]

On the morning of the third day, a man on the wall looked out at the Golden Horn, the way he had looked out at it every morning of his life, and saw masts.

Masts inside the chain.

[Pause. Let it land.]

There is a kind of silence that comes after seeing something impossible. Not fear. Not yet. Just the mind refusing, for a moment, to connect one thing to another.

He looked at the masts. He looked at the chain, still up, still unbroken. He looked back at the masts.

And then he went to wake the others.

[Pause.]

Inside the city, that morning, something changed that no wall had managed to change in a thousand years.

The water was no longer safe.

That was all. That was the whole of it. Not a breach. Not a battle. Just this: the one thing that had been certain, the one thing that had been true every single day of every single life in that city, was no longer true.

You could no longer say: whatever happens, the sea is ours.

[Long pause. Water. Let the bed breathe for ten seconds.]

Let us leave the Horn now. Let us walk back along the water, past the chain, past the quiet ships, up the stone steps, and into the city.

There is a lamp burning in a window on the hill. Someone is awake. Someone always is.

Let us go and see what they are doing.

### V · THE GUNS  [12:00 · ≈1,400 words]

[Low wind. Far away, a single dull knock — wood on wood.]

The answer to a wall is not a soldier. It is a machine.

For a thousand years, the walls of Constantinople had been the best answer anyone had ever built to the question of how to keep an army out. And for a thousand years, the answer to the walls had been the same: you cannot.

You can starve them. You can wait. You can try the gate at night. But you cannot knock the wall down. The stone is too thick. The men on the walls are too many. The ditch is too deep.

That was true for eleven hundred years.

Then someone invented a way to throw a very large stone a very long way, and everything that had been true stopped being true.

[Pause.]

Let us look at the machine.

It is not beautiful. That is the first thing. We imagine great engines of war as elegant things — carved, symmetrical, clever. This is not elegant. It is a tube.

A tube of bronze, cast in one piece, so long that a man cannot reach from one end of it to the other with both arms wide. It rests in a cradle of heavy timber, and the timber rests on a bed of stone and packed earth, and the whole thing is held down with chains and stakes and iron bands.

It is so large that it had to be built where it stands. It could not be carried. It could only be dragged, and even dragging it took sixty oxen and a road built for the purpose.

[Pause.]

The man who made it is not a soldier. He is a founder. His name is Urban — or something close to it, because names travel badly across languages — and he came to the city a year earlier, and offered his invention to the emperor.

The emperor had no money. Not the kind of money this would take.

So Urban went to the other side of the water, and offered it to the sultan.

The sultan had money.

[Soft pause.]

I want you to notice something about this. It is not a moral. It is just a fact, and it is the sort of fact that this whole night is made of.

A city runs out of money. A man crosses the water. A machine changes sides.

Nobody in this story is a villain. Everybody is doing a job, in the way that people do jobs, for whoever can pay for the work.

[Pause.]

The gun is fired by a team. There are perhaps a hundred men around it, though only a few of them touch it. There are men who carry the powder in wooden tubs lined with leather, and men who pack it with a wooden rammer, slowly and carefully, because a spark in the wrong place would kill everyone in the ring.

There are men who bring the stone.

The stone is the part I want to describe, because it is easy to forget.

It is not a small ball. It is a sphere cut from marble — or from granite, on the days when the marble runs out — so large that a man cannot put his arms around it, and so heavy that it takes a cart to bring it to the gun, and a crane to lift it into the mouth.

And here is the thing that is so strange that it is almost tender: the stones were made somewhere else, and brought here. The best ones came by sea, from an island in the Marmara. They were cut, and smoothed, and loaded onto boats, and sailed across the water in the night, to be fired at the city they came from.

[Pause.]

Now let us stand here for a moment, and think about the firing.

There is a flash. That is the obvious part, and it is over quickly.

The sound comes later. That is the part nobody expects. The sound of a great gun is not a bang. It is a blow. It hits you in the chest, and it goes on hitting you for a long time after the flash is gone, rolling out across the water and coming back off the hills, so that for a few seconds it is hard to tell which direction it came from.

And then there is the dust.

[Soft pause.]

After the sound, the air in front of the gun is full of dust, and the dust does not settle. It hangs, and it drifts, and it makes the whole morning the colour of a road.

The men around the gun do not cheer. They are already working. Someone is swabbing the bore with a wet cloth on a long pole — slowly, carefully, because this is the most dangerous moment of the whole process. Someone is measuring powder. Someone is walking out to look at where the stone went.

They will do this again in an hour.

And again after that.

[Pause.]

Let us walk to the wall now, and look from the other side.

This is harder. The men on the wall have been hearing this sound for weeks. They have learned to tell the great gun from the smaller ones. They have learned which sections of wall it is aiming at, and which of them are likely to be standing under it.

They have also learned what the stone does.

It does not knock a hole, the way we imagine. It does something slower.

The stone strikes the face of the wall, and the face of the wall crumbles. Not much — a few arms' lengths of stone, a section of facing, a great deal of dust. Then the next stone comes, an hour later, and crumbles a little more. And the next.

And under the facing, there is rubble. And under the rubble, there is earth. And the earth moves.

[Pause.]

That is what the men on the wall notice, and what they cannot stop. The wall is not being broken. It is being loosened.

Every night, they go out with baskets and barrels, and they fill the gaps, and they prop the loose stone with timber, and they build back a little of what the day took away. Every night, they do this in the dark, while the guns are quiet, with the sound of their own shovels very loud in the silence.

Every morning, the guns begin again.

[Long pause. Wind.]

There is one more thing to say about the guns, and then we will leave them.

They are not perfect. This is easy to forget, because we tell this story knowing how it ends. But on this night — the night of the twenty-eighth of May — the great gun is broken.

The mouth has split. That happens to bronze when it is asked to do more than bronze wants to do. The crack runs back from the lip, and every shot widens it, and the men who work it know that one day it will simply come apart in their hands.

So they have been repairing it. In the dark, on the ground, in the dirt, with iron rings heated red and hammered on, and ropes, and a great deal of argument.

They will finish tonight.

[Pause.]

I do not want you to think of this as a triumph. It is not a triumph. It is a group of tired men, in a field, in the dark, trying to make a broken thing work one more time.

We do that too. All of us. At some point in our lives, we are the men in the field with the broken thing, deciding whether it is worth another hour.

[Soft pause. Let the music hold.]

Let us go back into the city now. The lamps are still lit. There is still a little time.

### VI · THE LAST LITURGY  [14:00 · ≈1,500 words]

[Very soft interior. One low tone. Candles, if candles had a sound.]

There is a building in this city that has outlived everything.

It was built in the sixth century, by an emperor who wanted to make something that could not be forgotten. It has a dome so wide and so high that when it was finished, people said it was held up by a chain from heaven.

Its name is the Church of the Holy Wisdom. Hagia Sophia.

On this night, it is full.

[Pause.]

Let us go in.

The doors are open. They have been open all day. Inside, the first thing is not the light — it is the dark. The building is enormous, and the night has taken most of it. But there are lamps. Hundreds of them, on long chains, low and warm, so that the great gold ceiling above you is not lit at all. It is simply a presence in the dark. You know it is there because the sound behaves differently underneath it.

And then there is the smell.

Oil. Wax. Old stone. Incense, in a slow cloud. And under all of it, the smell of a great many people who have been standing in the same place for a long time.

[Soft pause.]

Now, look at the people.

This is the part I want you to see clearly, because it is the part the histories leave out.

They are not a crowd in the way we think of a crowd. They are not moving. They are not pushing. They are standing, or kneeling, or sitting on the stone floor with their backs against columns.

There are soldiers here, in armour, with their helmets off and held against their chests. There are old men. There are women with children asleep against their shoulders. There is a boy of perhaps nine, standing very straight, holding a candle that is much too big for him, and trying with all his strength to look as though he has done this many times before.

He has not.

[Pause.]

At the front, near the altar, there is the emperor.

Constantine the Eleventh. He is forty-nine years old. He has been emperor for four years, and in those four years he has had almost nothing but trouble. He has a city, and a title, and no empire worth the name.

He is not the figure you would paint. He is a man of middle height, with a beard going grey, standing in the light of a lamp with his hands folded, and his head a little bowed.

He is praying.

[Pause.]

Now, I want to be careful here, because this is a moment where it is easy to become grand, and grand is not what this night is.

This is not a scene from a painting. Nobody is being brave in an obvious way. Nobody is making a speech. There is no music swelling.

It is simply a great dark room, and a great many tired people, and the sound of a service that has been performed in this building for a thousand years, being performed one more time.

[Soft pause. Let the tone swell very slightly, then fall.]

The service goes on for a long time. It is meant to. It is not a thing you finish. It is a thing you are inside of.

Listen to it. Not to the words — the words are in a language most of the people in the room no longer speak. Listen to the shape of it. The rise and the fall. The long, low chanting that goes on until you stop noticing where it began.

This is the oldest technology in the world for making people calm. A dark room. A single tone. A great many people breathing together. A promise that something continues.

[Long pause.]

And then, near the end, something happens that the chroniclers remembered.

The emperor speaks.

Not a long speech. Not a proclamation. He asks the people in that room to forgive him. For his sins, for his failures, for all the things he could not do in four years with no money and no allies and a wall that was coming apart under a stone.

And the people answer him.

Nobody writes down the words. What they write down is the sound — that the whole building answered, and that it went on for some time.

[Pause.]

After that, the emperor goes out.

And here is what I want you to notice, and it is a small thing, and it is the most human thing in this whole night.

People follow him to the doors.

Not to ask him anything. Not to demand anything. Not to say goodbye, in a formal way. They simply follow him, the way you follow someone you are not ready to stop looking at.

[Soft pause.]

Now let us stay inside for a few minutes more, and let the room empty.

The people go out slowly. Nobody is in a hurry. The lamps begin to be put out, one by one, by people whose job it is. The great sound of the crowd becomes the sound of footsteps, and then a few voices, and then one voice, and then nothing.

And then the room is empty, and the last lamp is still burning.

[Long pause. Almost nothing.]

It is strange, being in a great building when it is empty. You become aware of small sounds. The wick. The stone. Somewhere, wind in a window that has been left open.

And you become aware, in a way that is hard to explain, of all the other nights that were like this one. A thousand years of evening services. A thousand years of people going out slowly, and the lamps being put out one by one.

Tonight, someone put out the lamps for the last time.

They did not know it. That is the thing. The person who put the lamp out last was just doing their job, at the end of a long day, and they wanted to go home.

[Pause.]

Let us go out into the square, and the cool air, and look back at the building.

It is still there. It is enormous, and dark, and it is still there.

In the morning it will be somebody else's. We know this. But tonight, it is still lit from within, and the doors are still open, and a few people are still standing in the square, not wanting to go home.

Let us stand with them.

[Long pause. Let the wind carry it.]

### VII · THE PEOPLE IN THE STREETS  [13:00 · ≈1,300 words]

[Bread ovens. Distant, quiet voices. A door closing.]

Later, historians will write about the siege.

They will write about the walls, and the guns, and the ships, and the emperor, and the numbers, and the dates. They will argue about how many men, and which gate, and which hour.

Nobody wrote about the bread.

[Pause.]

So let us write about it now, together, on our way through the streets.

It is late, but the bakeries are still working. Not all of them. Most are dark. But there is one street near the middle of the city where the ovens are still hot, and where a line has formed — not a long line, but a line of the kind that forms at the end of a very long day, when everyone in it knows they will be served eventually, and nobody has the energy to be impatient.

There is a woman at the front of the line with a cloth bag, and she is talking to the baker. Not about the morning. About her son, who is on the wall. About the fact that he does not like rye. The baker listens. He gives her a loaf and a half, and takes no money for the half.

Nobody says anything about this. It is not a scene. It is just what happens.

[Soft pause.]

Now walk on, up the hill, and look into the windows that are still lit.

Here is a family with five people in one room. Two of them are asleep, in the corner, on a pallet, under a cloak. One is mending a fishing net, though there is no fishing now and no boat to go in. One is sitting at a table with a bowl of water and a cloth, cleaning a wound on the hand of the fifth, who is tired and letting it happen.

The lamp is low. Nobody is talking. The room is warm.

This is the whole of it. This is what the histories leave out. On the last night of the oldest empire in the world, most people are doing the smallest possible things, in the smallest possible rooms, and they are doing them with a gentleness that would not seem possible if we did not do them ourselves.

[Pause.]

Let us go on.

There is a church here — not the great one, a small one, in a side street, with a door that has been worn in the middle by five centuries of hands. It is full. Not crowded — full. There is no room to stand, and yet nobody is at the door, because in this city, on this night, a church door is the one place where there is always room.

Most of the people inside are women and old men and children.

And here is a thing that I want you to notice, and it is not sentimental, it is simply true: they are not afraid. Or if they are, they have stopped behaving like it.

Fear is a fast thing. It makes people hurry. It makes them speak quickly, and look around, and make plans.

There is none of that in this room. There is the opposite. There is the slowness of people who have put something down.

[Soft pause.]

Outside, in the street, three soldiers are sitting on a low wall.

They are not on duty. Their armour is off, and piled beside them, and one of them is eating something out of a cloth. They are talking about a town one of them comes from, and a fight they had there when they were boys, and one of them is laughing — a real laugh, not a brave one.

Let us leave them to it.

[Pause.]

Now, there is one more thing in the street, and then we will go back inside.

At the end of a narrow lane, near the southern wall, there is a house that is not a house. It is a garden, with a small building in it, and the building is full of paper.

Not money. Not records. Paper of every kind: scrolls in leather cases, codices stacked on shelves, loose leaves in boxes, and on the floor, neat piles of things that were taken out of boxes today and have not yet been put back.

The city is full of these places. Most of them are not famous. They are the private libraries of people who loved books, in a city where books were still copied by hand, one letter at a time, by someone sitting in a window with a good light.

There are people in this garden tonight, and they are working.

They are not praying. They are choosing.

[Soft pause.]

This is the part of the night that matters most for us — for this channel, for these logs, for all the paper that has ever been saved from anything.

Somebody, in that garden, is deciding what to take.

Not everything. That has been clear for weeks. What you can carry, in a small boat, with no guarantee that the boat will arrive, is not very much.

So: which ones?

The oldest? The most beautiful? The ones nobody else has a copy of? The ones you have not read yet? The ones that belonged to your father?

I have thought about this a great deal. Not because it is dramatic, but because it is exactly what a person does when they know they are leaving a place forever, and they have one bag.

[Long pause. Paper. A page turning.]

We will come back to these people at the end of the night.

For now, let us walk away, up the hill, where the street is wider and the wind gets in.

There is a fountain here, in the middle of a small square. It is running. It has been running for a thousand years, more or less, and tonight somebody has tied a cup to it with a piece of string, so that anyone who passes can drink.

Nobody knows who did that.

That is the whole of this city, in one cup on a string. Somebody, at some point today, thought: people will be walking past here, and they will be thirsty, and there should be a cup.

[Pause.]

Let us drink, and let us sit on the edge of the fountain for a while, and let the night go past us.

It is not a long night. But there is still some of it left.
