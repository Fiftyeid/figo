# THE LAST NIGHT OF CONSTANTINOPLE — INTERLUDES
## Three chapters that sit between the main movements
### A · THE WATER BELOW  /  B · THE NAMES IN THE STONE  /  C · THE SCRIPTORIUM

**Series:** The Lost Nights · Episode 01
**Voice:** the Keeper — low, close, unhurried.
**Rule:** nothing here repeats a fact already told. Each interlude is a room the story walks through, not a summary of it.

### A · THE WATER BELOW  [≈15:00 · ≈1,800 words]

[Low drip, slow. Stone room tone. No wind.]

Before we go any further into this night, I want to take you somewhere the story does not usually go.

Down.

Under the streets. Under the houses. Under the lamps and the prayers and the men on the wall.

[Pause.]

There is a city underneath this city, and almost nobody in it has ever seen the whole of it. Not the emperor. Not the patriarch. Not the general who commands the walls. There are men who know one staircase, one corridor, one flooded room, and have known it their whole lives, and have never once been able to draw you a map.

We are going to walk down a hundred and something steps, and stand in the dark, and listen.

[Pause. Water.]

The first thing you notice is the cold. It comes up the stairs to meet you, the way cold does, like something alive that has been waiting.

The second thing you notice is the smell. Wet stone. Not rot. Stone does not rot. It is a clean, mineral smell, old and patient, and it is the smell of a place that has been wet for a thousand years and does not intend to stop.

The third thing you notice is that you can hear the city, but only a little. The noise of the street comes down the stairwell thin and far away, the way a voice comes through a wall. Everything else is water.

[Pause.]

Now. Look up.

You are standing in a forest. Not a real forest. A forest of columns, hundreds of them, rising out of black water, and every one of them is carrying a ceiling you cannot see. Some of them are carved. Some of them are plain. Some of them are not columns at all, in the way a column is supposed to be a column. One of them has a face on it. One of them is standing upside down.

They were brought here from somewhere else.

That is the thing I want you to hold on to. Every one of these columns used to be part of a temple, or a triumphal arch, or a bath, or a palace that nobody remembers. When this cistern was built, the city was already old, and the builders did what builders have always done. They used what was there. They took the columns down, and rolled them here, and stood them up in the dark, and put a roof on them, and filled the space with water.

The temple is gone. The column is still working.

[Soft pause.]

There are, in this city, more than a hundred cisterns. Some of them are small enough to be a rich man's cellar. Some of them are the size of a church. There are open cisterns too, great square holes in the ground that the people call by the name of whatever grows in them now, because a thing that holds water in one century holds cabbages in the next.

And there is an aqueduct, crossing the middle of the city on arches so tall that a child can walk under them and feel the size of the sky. It brings water from the hills, from rivers and springs a long way out, along a line of stone that men have been repairing, and repairing, and repairing, since before anyone now alive was born.

[Pause.]

A city is not only a place where people live. It is a machine for staying alive, and most of that machine is out of sight.

You can see the walls. The walls are the part everyone photographs, in the centuries when there are cameras. But the walls are the least interesting part of the machine, because the walls only decide how a city dies. The water decides whether it lives.

[Pause.]

Let us think about the numbers for a moment, in the way the men who ran this city had to think about them.

Nine thousand people. Perhaps a little more. Perhaps a little less. Nobody knows, because nobody has ever counted properly, and on a night like this nobody is going to start.

Nine thousand people need water. Not a great deal of it, by the standards of a river. But they need it every day, and they need it to be clean, because a city without water does not simply get thirsty. It gets sick. It gets a fever. It gets the kind of death that moves from house to house without knocking.

[Soft pause.]

So there is a rule in this city, and it is older than any emperor, and it is written down nowhere, because everyone knows it.

Before a siege, you fill the cisterns.

Not when the army appears. Before. When the rumours start. When the men who sell horses begin to leave. When the price of grain does a thing that grain should not do in a quiet month.

You fill them slowly, because water takes time to move, and you fill them quietly, because panic is faster than water.

And then you close the lids, and you put a lock on the lid, and you give the key to a man who has been given keys his whole life and has never lost one.

[Pause.]

Tonight, the cisterns are full.

I want you to picture that. Under the streets, under the churches, under the houses where people are trying to sleep, there is a second city made entirely of water. Still. Black. Cold. Holding a month, perhaps two months, of life in the dark.

And it is completely silent, except for one sound.

[Pause. Water.]

The drip.

You will hear it before you see anything. It is not a leak. It is not a fault. It is the sound of a full cistern breathing. Water gathers on the ceiling, on the old brick, on the underside of a roof that was laid by men who are now only names on a stone somewhere, and it lets go, and it falls, and it lands.

And the sound it makes in a room like this is the most patient sound in the world.

[Pause.]

The men who check the cisterns come down once a night.

There are three of them. One carries the lamp. One carries a long stick with a mark cut into it, a notch at the height the water should be. One carries nothing at all, and does the counting of the steps, because he has done it so many times that he can tell, by the number of steps, whether they have come down the right stair.

They do not talk much. The room does not want them to. Their voices come back at them off the water with a delay, and a man who hears his own voice come back gets quiet very fast.

[Pause.]

They walk along the edge. The lamp makes a small orange circle on the water and the circle does not stay still, because the water is not still, even when it looks still. It shivers. It has its own small weather.

The man with the stick lowers it in. He finds the bottom. He lifts it out. He holds it beside the lamp and looks at the wet line, and he compares the wet line to the notch.

And then he says one word, to the other two, and the word is usually the same word, and the word means: enough.

[Soft pause.]

Tonight, the word is enough.

[Pause.]

I like these three men. I like them very much. They are not heroes. Nobody will write about them. If the city survives, they will go back to their ordinary work and nobody will ever ask them what they did on the night of the twenty-eighth of May.

But they are the ones who make the rest of the night possible.

Because everything that happens up there, on the wall, in the church, in the streets, in the palace, depends on this: that when a man is thirsty at three in the morning, there is water, and it is close, and he does not have to leave his post to find it.

That is what a civilisation is, when you take it apart. Not the gold. Not the crowns. Not the ceremonies.

Somebody, somewhere, has arranged for the water to be there.

[Pause.]

Now let us go a little deeper. There is one more thing down here, and it is the thing that makes this place more than a cellar.

Somewhere in the dark, past the columns, past the end of the lamp's reach, there is a wall that is not a wall of this cistern.

It is older. It is brick, and it is very thick, and it goes up past where the ceiling should be. It is the foundation of something that used to stand here before the cistern was dug. A temple, perhaps. A house. Nobody is sure.

And in the wet brick, at about the height of a man's chest, there is a hole.

It is not a big hole. It is the size of two fists. It was made by men, not by water, and it was made recently, because the edges are still sharp.

[Pause.]

I want to be careful here, because this is the part of the night where the story usually turns into a ghost story, and I do not want to give you a ghost. I want to give you something worse, and better, and much more ordinary.

There are men on the other side of that wall.

They are digging.

[Long pause. Water.]

They have been digging for weeks. They are not trying to get into the cistern. They are trying to get under the wall, out there, the great wall, the one with the emperor's name on it, and they are using the old drains and the old foundations as a road, because a road underground is a road nobody is watching.

And the men of the city are digging too, from this side, with their ears.

That is what the hole is for.

[Pause.]

They put their faces close to it, and they listen, and they try to work out which way the sound is going, and how far away it is, and whether it is under the wall or under the street or under the church.

And when they are sure, they dig their own tunnel, and they fill it with fire, and they light it, and they run.

[Soft pause.]

I am not going to describe what happens then. This is not that kind of night, and this is not that kind of channel.

But I want you to notice what it means.

It means that this siege is not only happening above, where we can see it. It is happening below, in the dark, in the water, in the brick, in the space between two foundations, where two groups of tired men are listening to each other through a wall and trying to guess which of them will be surprised.

[Pause.]

The man with the lamp does not go near the hole. He has been told not to. He is not a soldier and he is not a mason and his job is the water.

But he hears it, once, as they turn to go back up. A sound through the brick. Not loud. A scrape, and then a pause, and then another scrape.

And he stands still for a moment with the lamp in his hand, and the water shivering around his feet, and he thinks a thought that I would like to give him, because it seems to me the most human thought in this whole city.

He thinks: they are digging in the dark, too.

[Long pause.]

That is the thing about this night that I keep coming back to. Everyone in it is in the dark. The emperor does not know what the sultan is doing. The sultan does not know what the emperor is doing. The men on the wall do not know what the men under the wall are doing. The men in the boats do not know what the men on the hill are doing. And every one of them is doing the best they can with the little that they can see.

[Pause.]

Let us go back up.

The stairs are steep and there are a hundred and something of them, and by the time you reach the top your legs are burning and the air smells of dust and smoke and the ordinary world.

Behind you, the door closes.

And under the street, in the dark, the water keeps its level, and the drip keeps its time, and the city keeps its second clock, which nobody winds and nobody stops.

[Pause. Water fades.]

### B · THE NAMES IN THE STONE  [≈15:00 · ≈1,800 words]

[Chisel on stone, distant. Wind on a high place.]

Come with me to the wall.

Not the top of it, where the men stand. The bottom. The place where the wall meets the ground, and where, if you look closely, you can see how it was made.

[Pause.]

Most people look at a wall and see a wall. A flat thing. A thing that stops you.

A mason looks at a wall and sees a hundred decisions.

This stone came from here. That one came from there. This one is new, cut last year from the quarry beyond the hill. That one is old, much older than the wall, and it has been in three buildings before this one, and if you turn it over you will find a name on it.

[Pause.]

That is what I want to show you tonight. Not the wall as a barrier. The wall as a library.

[Soft pause.]

Here is a block, at about the height of your shoulder, on the inner face, where the mortar has worn away and the stone is showing.

Look at the edge. There are letters.

They are Greek, and they are old, older than the wall, older than the empire that built the wall. They were cut by a man with a chisel and a steady hand, in a workshop somewhere in this city, for a building that has since fallen down. Perhaps a temple. Perhaps a monument to a charioteer. Perhaps the base of a statue of a man who was important for about eleven years and is now completely forgotten.

The stone was cut to fit that building. Then the building went. Then the stone was carried here, and turned, and set into the wall, and the letters were put face down, or face in, where nobody would have to look at them.

Except that the mortar has worn away, and now you are looking at them.

[Pause.]

There is a word for this, and it is a good word, and it comes from Latin. Spolia. The spoils. The things taken from one place and used in another.

The Romans did it. The Byzantines did it. Every city that has ever been built on top of another city has done it, because stone is heavy and men are tired and the cheapest quarry in the world is the building next door that nobody is using anymore.

[Soft pause.]

But I do not think it is only economics.

I think there is something else in it, and I want to say it carefully, because it is easy to make it sound like poetry when it is really just a fact about labour.

When you build a wall out of the past, you are making a promise. You are saying: this thing will hold, and it will hold because it is made of things that have already held.

A stone that has stood for four hundred years in a temple is a stone that knows how to stand.

[Pause.]

There is a story — and I should tell you that it is a story, not a record — that when the walls were being repaired in the last years, before this night, the masons found a block with an inscription that nobody could read, and the foreman told them to turn it face in and set it, and one of the younger men asked why, and the foreman said: because a wall should not argue with the men who live behind it.

I do not know if that happened. I hope it did. It sounds like something a foreman would say.

[Pause.]

Let us walk along the base of the wall. Slowly.

Here is a column, set into the masonry on its side, so that the fluting runs horizontally, like a row of small hills. Here is a capital, a Corinthian capital, with the leaves still sharp, doing the work of a common block, holding up nothing but more wall.

Here is a piece of a frieze. A battle. Men with spears, and horses, and a border of little flowers. It is upside down.

[Soft pause.]

Somebody carved that. Somebody stood in front of a block of marble with a mallet and a chisel and made a horse, and it took him weeks, and he was proud of it, and he signed it at the bottom in letters the size of a fingernail.

And now it is upside down at knee height, in the dark, in a wall, holding up more wall.

[Pause.]

I want you to sit with that for a moment, because it is not a sad thought. It is not even a thought about loss. It is a thought about how long a city actually is.

A city is not one generation. It is not one empire. It is not even one language. It is a pile of things that people made, and the pile keeps getting used, and every so often somebody turns a block over and finds a name.

[Pause.]

There are, in this city, statues that nobody can identify. There are bronze horses on a pedestal above the harbour, and nobody now alive knows where they came from or who made them or what they were for. There are columns with emperors on top who have been mistaken for gods, and gods who have been mistaken for emperors, and at least one statue that the people of the city believe has a prophecy attached to it, which is the most flattering thing you can do to a piece of stone.

[Soft pause.]

And there are the names.

That is what I keep coming back to. Not the statues. The names.

On the base of a column, near the ground, in letters worn almost smooth, there is a name. It might be the name of the man who paid for the column. It might be the name of the man who carved it. It might be the name of a saint, or a horse, or a workshop, or a joke.

Nobody knows. Nobody in the city can read it now. It is too worn, and the language is too old, and the men who could have read it died a long time ago.

But the name is there, and every night, the men who walk this stretch of wall walk past it, and their shadows fall on it, and it is still a name.

[Pause. Wind.]

Let us go up onto the wall now. There is a stair at the corner, and the steps are worn in the middle, the way steps get worn, by a thousand years of the same footfall in the same place.

At the top, the wind.

[Pause.]

From up here you can see the whole shape of the thing. The wall running away from you to the north, and to the south, and the towers at intervals, and the ditch, and beyond the ditch the ground where the enemy is, and beyond that the dark.

And you can see that the wall is not one wall. It is a hundred walls. It is repairs on repairs on repairs. Look at the colour of the stone. Here it is grey. Here it is yellow. Here it is the pale, almost-white colour of a marble that came from an island that is now part of somebody else's empire. Here is a patch of brick, and here is a patch of rubble, and here is a patch of stone that was cut last month and is still pale, and still sharp, and does not match anything.

[Pause.]

The wall is a sentence that has been written and rewritten so many times that it is no longer a sentence. It is a palimpsest. Do you know that word?

A palimpsest is a page that has been scraped clean and written on again. And if you hold it up to the light, you can see the old writing under the new writing, faint, patient, still there.

That is what this wall is. A page that has been scraped and written and scraped and written for eleven hundred years.

And tonight, for the first time in eleven hundred years, the writing is about to be scraped off.

[Long pause. Wind.]

I want to end this interlude with a small thing, because the big things are coming later and you should have a small thing to hold.

There is a mason on this wall tonight. He is not a soldier. He has been kept on because the wall always needs mending, and tonight it needs mending more than usual, and he is up here with a basket of mortar and a trowel and a lamp that he keeps behind a stone so that it does not shine over the parapet.

He is working on a gap. The gap was made by a stone from the great gun, three days ago. It is not a bad gap. It is the kind of gap that a mason can fix in a night, if he is left alone.

[Pause.]

He sets a block. He taps it with the handle of the trowel. He checks it with his thumb.

And then he turns to the basket for the next block, and the next block is too big, so he takes his chisel and he trims the edge, and when he turns it over to see the face he finds letters.

Greek letters. Old. A name.

[Soft pause.]

He looks at them for a moment. He has seen this a hundred times. Every mason in this city has seen this a hundred times.

And then he does what masons do, which is the most human thing in this whole night.

He sets the block with the letters facing in, and he mortars the edge, and he taps it down, and he goes on to the next one.

[Pause.]

Because the wall has to hold.

Not because the name does not matter. It matters. It is the reason he paused. But the wall has to hold, and the name has held for a thousand years already, and it can hold a little longer, in the dark, with its face turned in.

[Long pause. Wind.]

Somewhere tonight, a man is putting a name back into the wall.

Somewhere in a hundred years, another man will take it out again, and look at it, and wonder who it was.

That is how a city survives. Not by remembering. By being built out of things that were remembered, and then forgotten, and then found again, by somebody with a trowel and a lamp and no time at all.

[Pause. Wind fades.]

### C · THE SCRIPTORIUM  [≈15:00 · ≈1,800 words]

[One low tone. A page turning. No wind.]

Now let us go where the city keeps its memory.

Not the palace. The palace keeps the seals and the gold and the orders. Memory is kept somewhere else.

[Pause.]

It is a room behind a church, on the second floor, with a window that faces north, because a north window gives a flat, even, unromantic light, and a man who is copying a manuscript does not want romance. He wants to see the letters.

There are eleven men in the room. Some of them are monks. Some of them are not — there is a notary, and a boy of about fourteen who is learning, and an old man whose hands shake until he picks up a pen, and then do not shake at all.

Nobody is talking. There is a rule about talking, and the rule is not about holiness. It is about ink.

[Pause.]

Let me tell you what the room sounds like, because the room is one of the quietest places in this city, and this is a night when everything else is loud.

You hear the pen. Not a scratch. A soft, dry, continuous whisper, like a small animal walking on paper. Eleven of them, at different speeds, in different parts of the room, so that the sound moves around you the way rain moves around a roof.

You hear the page, when somebody turns it. That is a bigger sound than you expect. It is a whole event. It has a beginning and an end.

You hear somebody shift on a stool. You hear somebody breathe out through the nose, slowly, the way a man breathes when he has just finished a difficult line.

And you hear, very faintly, from outside, the city.

[Pause.]

The city sounds different from up here. From the street you hear the market, and the arguments, and the wheels. From the second floor you hear the shape of it: a low continuous hum, without words, the sound of a great many people doing small things.

Tonight, the hum is wrong.

It is too quiet in some places and too loud in others. There is a gap where the market should be. There is a noise near the wall that is not a wheel and not a cart.

But the men in the room have decided, some time in the last few weeks, not to listen to it.

[Soft pause.]

That is not cowardice. It is a discipline, and it is the oldest discipline in this room, and it has a name in the rule of the monastery. It means: do the work in front of you.

[Pause.]

Let us look at the work.

The page is not paper, in the way you and I mean paper. It is parchment, which is animal skin, which has been soaked, and scraped, and stretched on a frame, and dried, and rubbed with chalk, and cut. It takes a whole sheep to make a book of a certain size, and it takes a hundred sheep to make a book of a certain other size, and that is one of the reasons why books are expensive, and why a book is sometimes the most valuable object in a house.

The ink is not ink, in the way you and I mean ink. It is oak gall and iron and gum, boiled together, and it is brown when it is new and black when it dries, and it bites into the skin, and it will still be readable in five hundred years if the book is kept dry.

[Pause.]

The pen is a reed, cut at an angle, with a slit in the nib. A man cuts his own. He cuts it with a small knife, and he cuts it often, because a reed goes soft, and a soft reed makes a fat line, and a fat line is a mistake.

There is no such thing as crossing out.

[Soft pause.]

I want you to feel the weight of that sentence, because it is the whole of this room.

There is no such thing as crossing out.

If you make a mistake, you have three choices. You can scrape the ink off the skin with a knife and write again, and hope that the surface takes it. You can write the mistake into the margin, with a small mark, and let the next man who copies the book deal with it. Or you can leave it, and let it stand, and hope that nobody notices for four hundred years.

Most of the time, they leave it.

[Pause.]

That is why the old books are full of small human things. A word spelled two ways in the same line. A line that jumps. A gap where a word should be, because the copyist's eye slipped from one line to the one below it, and he did not notice until the page was dry.

And here and there, in the margin, in a different hand, a note.

Not a correction. A comment. A complaint about the cold. A prayer for a brother who is ill. A small drawing of a bird.

[Soft pause.]

There is a copyist in this city — not in this room, in another, years ago — who wrote in the margin of a very long book: I am cold, and the light is bad, and I have been at this since the third hour. If you are reading this, pray for me.

Somebody is reading it. Right now, in a library in another country, somebody is reading it.

That is the whole story of this interlude, really. Everything else is detail.

[Pause.]

Back to the room. Back to tonight.

The work in front of these eleven men is not the copying of a whole book. Nobody starts a whole book in the last week of May. A book is a project of months, sometimes years, and a man who starts one now knows that he will not finish it.

What they are doing is smaller and stranger.

They are copying the things that are not in books.

[Pause.]

A list of the relics in a church, and where each one is kept, and which of them can be moved. A list of the debts of a monastery. A list of the names of the men who have agreed to carry the archives out of the city if it becomes necessary, and where they will meet, and what they will say to the man at the gate.

A letter from a friend in Italy, copied so that there are two copies, one to keep and one to send.

And a page of notes — just notes, not a chronicle — of what has happened in the last eight weeks, in order, with dates, in a small careful hand, because a man in this room has decided that somebody should write it down while it is happening.

[Soft pause.]

That man is not a historian. He is a deacon, and he is tired, and he has a cold, and he writes the notes in the last hour before dawn, when the others have gone to their cells and the room is his.

He writes them as if someone will read them.

He does not write them as if he will survive.

[Long pause.]

Let me tell you what he wrote last night. Not the words — the sense of it, because I want to keep the words for later, and because the words belong to him, not to me.

He wrote that the city was quiet. He wrote that the moon was down. He wrote that there had been firing in the afternoon and none since the second hour of the night. He wrote that a man had come to the door asking for bread, and had been given bread, and had gone away without saying thank you, and that this was not rudeness, it was exhaustion.

And then he wrote, in the margin, in a smaller hand:

[Pause.]

We are all very tired, and the ink is running low, and I do not know what will be left of this.

[Soft pause.]

That is the sentence I would keep, if I could keep only one sentence from this whole night. Not because it is beautiful. Because it is true, and it is small, and it was written by a man who did not know that anyone would ever read it.

And here is the thing that makes it more than a sad note in a margin.

It is still here.

[Pause.]

The ink ran low. The city fell. The man who wrote it is dust. And the sentence is still here, in a book, in a library, and I am reading it to you now, on a night six hundred years later, in a voice that did not exist when he wrote it.

[Soft pause.]

That is what a scriptorium is for. Not the books. The books are the by-product. The thing a scriptorium is for is the survival of a sentence.

[Pause.]

Let us leave them to it. There is nothing more to see, and a room like that does not want an audience. The lamp is low. The boy is asleep with his head on his arms, and the notary is going to wake him in a moment, gently, because the boy has to learn the letters before he learns the hours.

And the deacon is writing, in the last hour before dawn, in a hand that is getting smaller as the page fills.

[Long pause.]

Before we go, one small thing.

On the shelf by the door, there is a book that nobody is copying. It is large, and it is old, and it has a cover of tooled leather that has been repaired so many times that the repairs are now part of the design.

It is a chronicle. It is the record of this city from the beginning, or from as close to the beginning as the men who started it could get.

And in the back of it, on the last page, there is a space.

A blank page. Not a page that was never written. A page that was left, deliberately, at the end of the book, because the men who made the book knew that a chronicle is never finished, and that somebody, someday, would need a page.

[Soft pause.]

That page is still blank tonight.

And it is the most frightening object in this room, and the most hopeful, and I want you to remember it, because we are going to come back to it.

[Pause. Page turns. Tone fades.]
