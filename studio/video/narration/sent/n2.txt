The wall of Constantinople is not one wall. It is a system. If you have heard of it, you have probably heard the number: the Theodosian Walls, built in the fifth century, eleven hundred years before this night, and still, in 1453, the strongest fortification in the world. But numbers do not tell you what it feels like to stand beside it. So let us stand beside it. The first thing you notice is that the wall is not flat. It has a rhythm. It steps. Every few dozen paces, a tower pushes out from the line, and the line steps forward, and the tower gives the defenders a place to stand and look along the face of the wall instead of straight out at the enemy. This is not decoration. This is a machine made of stone. Behind the tower, the wall goes on, and behind the wall, there is another wall. And behind that, another. And in front of everything, there is a ditch. A moat, wide and deep, cut into the ground so that anyone attacking has to climb down before they can climb up.
