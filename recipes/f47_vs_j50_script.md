## Script

### vo_intro
The F-47 versus the J-50. One of these aircraft flew last year. The other has a contract, a factory, and an engine that has already run on a test stand. So which side is actually ahead?

### vo_sec_01
Three aircraft. Not one of them in service. The F-47 is the American answer, still in production. The J-50 and the J-36 both flew in December twenty twenty-four, which makes them the only sixth-generation machines in the world that anyone has actually seen moving.

### vo_f47
Boeing F-47. This is the jet the US Air Force picked in twenty twenty-five, and the first aircraft is already in production. No official photograph exists. Every image you have seen is an artist's guess, including this one.

### vo_j50
Shenyang J-50. Flown in public, filmed by civilians, and still officially unexplained. China has not published a single specification, which has not stopped analysts from writing thousands of pages about it.

### vo_j36
Chengdu J-36. The third aircraft in the argument, and the one that changes it. Bigger, three engines, built for range rather than dogfighting. If the J-50 is a fighter, the J-36 is a flying battery.

### vo_sec_02
Both are tailless. Both threw away the vertical tail to stay invisible across more radar bands. That costs control authority, and each side solves it differently: the F-47 with canards, the J-50 with swivelling wingtips.

### vo_f47_airframe_ste1
The F-47 shape. Tailless, with canards doing the work the tail used to do. The Air Force says it is stealthier than anything it has flown, which is a claim, not a measurement.

### vo_j50_airframe_ste1
The J-50 shape. Also tailless, but the moving surfaces are at the wingtips, hinging like a bird's feathers. It is a more elegant solution, and much harder to make work.

### vo_sec_03
This is the category where the gap is documented. The American adaptive engines have run on test stands for years. The Chinese side is flying with engines that are powerful, but not yet a generation ahead.

### vo_f47_propulsion2
Two engines, three streams of air, and the ability to keep a jet cool while it runs flat out. The adaptive engine programme is the quiet American lead, and it is real hardware, not a slide.

### vo_j50_propulsion2
The J-50 flies on two engines with thrust vectoring, which buys manoeuvre. What it does not yet buy is range, and cooling headroom for a laser or a very large radar. That is the honest gap.

### vo_sec_04
Sensors and autonomy. This is the part of the comparison where both air forces stop talking, because the advantage is no longer in the airframe, it is in the network behind it.

### vo_f47_sensors_auto3
The F-47 is designed from the start to lead a formation of uncrewed aircraft. The pilot stops being a gunfighter and becomes a commander, with three or four cheap machines doing the dangerous part.

### vo_j50_sensors_auto3
China is running the same idea, with one difference that matters: its drones and its fighters come from the same closed supply chain, so integration is easier and nobody outside can see it working.

### vo_sec_05
Programmes. Money moves faster than metal, and this is where the two systems look least alike.

### vo_f47_the_programm4
A contract was awarded in twenty twenty-five. Aircraft are in production. That sounds like a lead, and in the boring industrial sense, it is: money has already turned into metal.

### vo_j50_the_programm4
Two flying prototypes and no published budget. Which sounds slower, until you remember that China built both of them between two December holiday photographs, without a press conference.

### vo_sec_06
So who is ahead? Let us keep it honest.

### vo_f47_the_verdict5
So the F-47 wins on engines and on industrial momentum. Its risk is the same one that has killed American programmes before: cost, and a schedule that keeps moving to the right.

### vo_j50_the_verdict5
The J-50 wins on one thing nobody in Washington likes to say out loud: it already flies. Its risk is engines and opacity — a black box is a great secret and a terrible supply chain.

### vo_outro
So which is really ahead? Neither, yet. Three prototypes are flying or building, none of them is in service, and the first squadron is still about five years away. The jet that wins will be the one whose country can keep it fed.
